\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Food production and the energy transition compete for the same land. Terawatts of new solar photovoltaic (PV) capacity are needed, ground-mounted solar farms occupy one to two hectares per megawatt, and croplands are where solar potential is greatest \citep{adeh2019}. At the same time, warming is projected to reduce yields of maize, wheat, rice and soybean by several per cent per degree \citep{zhao2017}. Agrivoltaic (AV) systems, which put crops and PV modules on the same land, aim to resolve this conflict \citep{dupraz2011,dinesh2016}. By sharing sunlight between modules and plants, a hectare can deliver more food plus energy than the two uses side by side, with land-equivalent ratios (LER) above one in field trials and models \citep{amaducci2018,valle2017,trommsdorff2021}, and the partial shade can lower canopy temperature and conserve soil water \citep{marrou2013,adeh2018,weselek2021}. The same shade also reduces the light available for photosynthesis, and regulators increasingly require AV systems to retain most of the reference yield; the German pre-standard DIN SPEC 91434 sets a floor of 66\% \citep{din2021}, and other schemes and farmers set stricter ones. How much electricity an AV system can produce under such a yield floor depends on the crop, the climate, the year and the growth stage.

Most AV designs fix the array geometry, the ground coverage ratio (GCR, module area per ground area), from rules of thumb. Single-axis trackers add a degree of freedom: modules can be rotated away from the sun to let light through when the crop needs it \citep{valle2017}, or towards the sun to maximise electricity. We call the daily choice between the two \emph{light sharing}. It is a sequential decision problem, because the value of light depends on the crop stage, soil water and weather, and the consequence appears at harvest. Machine learning for crop management has mostly used reinforcement learning with black-box simulators \citep{gautron2022}, which would need very many simulated seasons for global multi-decadal assessments. Differentiable process models, which fit parameters and policies by gradient descent through a simulator \citep{reichstein2019,shen2023}, are an alternative. A second difficulty is uncertainty about the crop response to shade, the weakest part of any AV model, which calls for designs that perform acceptably across plausible models and not only under the best estimate \citep{marchau2019}.

This paper asks three questions of a global set of agricultural systems. How much of the electricity that an AV system can produce under a yield floor comes from array density, and how much from dynamic light-sharing control of trackers? Does a controller learned through a differentiable simulator add anything to a well-designed hand-written rule? And how much does uncertainty about the shade response cost, and can learning help to manage it? To answer them we built a differentiable simulator that couples hourly solar geometry, tracker and PV physics, light and microclimate under the modules, and the SIMPLE crop model \citep{zhao2019} extended with a flowering-stage shade penalty. We trained a neural controller (SALS) through it, and compared it with static arrays and with rules, in a protocol in which the array density of every site is chosen from its own history only and performance is measured on geographically held-out sites and years. The shade response was learned from the open data of a meta-analysis \citep{laub2022}, checked against field trials \citep{gonocruz2021,weselek2021,ramosfuentes2023}, and propagated as an ensemble of plausible responses. We applied the protocol to 400 crop--site pairs sampled in proportion to the harvested area of the five crops \citep{yu2020}, with NASA POWER weather for 2001--2020 (19 sowing years) \citep{sparks2018} under baseline and +1.5, +2 and +3\,\textdegree C climates. The main findings are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Light sharing, by rule and not by learning, is the main lever on electricity at a given floor: arrays carry 2.1 to 2.9 times the module area of static arrays at the same mean retention of 90\%, but economically it pays only at high electricity prices.
\item A learned controller adds 0.044 LER over the written phenology rule but only 0.007 over a window optimised on the training seasons, and nothing that can be distinguished from zero at matched retention.
\item The shade response is only partly learnable: a hierarchical model fitted to 428 observations predicts a new study to about 0.22 in relative yield, no better than proportional loss for the four staple crop types. The simulator is milder than the data for maize.
\item Compliance with the floor is sensitive to the response assumed in the design. A density chosen under the mean response leaves about 70\% of pairs at the floor when another plausible response holds. A conformal margin attains a stated compliance inside the ensemble, and a neural design policy does no better than a simple margin; neither is safe outside the ensemble.
\item With illustrative costs, light sharing breaks even with the open field at 70 to 92\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$, and agrivoltaics on 5\% of the world's staple cropland could supply about 14\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ only with about 10\,TWp of modules.
\end{enumerate}
