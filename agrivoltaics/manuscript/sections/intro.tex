\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Two of the defining challenges of this century compete for the same resource: land. The energy transition requires terawatts of new solar photovoltaic (PV) capacity, and ground-mounted solar farms need roughly one to two hectares per megawatt. Food production must rise while crops are exposed to more frequent heat waves and droughts. Warming alone is projected to reduce global yields of maize, wheat, rice and soybean by several per cent per degree \citep{zhao2017,ipcc2021}, and a new generation of climate and crop models shows these impacts emerging earlier than previously expected \citep{jagermeyr2021}. Croplands are also where solar power potential is greatest \citep{adeh2019}. They are flat, sunny, accessible and close to rural electricity demand, so converting farmland into solar farms is tempting and controversial at the same time.

Agrivoltaic (AV) systems combine crops and PV modules on the same land and aim to resolve this conflict \citep{dupraz2011,dinesh2016,weselek2019}. By sharing sunlight between modules and plants, a hectare can deliver more food plus energy than the two uses side by side, with land-equivalent ratios (LER) well above one in field trials and modelling studies \citep{dupraz2011,amaducci2018,valle2017,trommsdorff2021}. The partial shade also changes the crop microclimate. It lowers canopy temperature and evaporative demand, conserves soil moisture and improves water-use efficiency, especially in drylands \citep{marrou2013,adeh2018,barron2019,elamri2018,weselek2021}. Agrivoltaics is therefore a candidate adaptation measure against heat and drought stress, as reviewed by \citet{mamun2022}, in addition to a mitigation measure through renewable electricity. The same shade, however, also reduces the light available for photosynthesis. Regulators increasingly require AV systems to retain most of the reference crop yield. The German pre-standard DIN SPEC 91434, for example, requires at least 66\% of the reference yield \citep{din2021}, and other schemes and many farmers set stricter floors. How much electricity can be produced without sacrificing food security depends on the crop, the local climate, the year and the growth stage.

Most AV designs fix the geometry of the array, i.e.\ the ground coverage ratio (GCR, module area per ground area) and the tilt or tracking rule, from generic rules of thumb. Single-axis trackers add a degree of freedom: the modules can be rotated away from the sun to let light through when the crop needs it, as demonstrated for lettuce by \citet{valle2017}, or towards the sun to maximise electricity. Choosing the rotation every day is a sequential decision problem. The value of light depends on the crop's phenological stage, soil water and upcoming weather, and the effect of a decision appears in the harvest months later. Recent work in this journal has used evolutionary multi-objective optimisation to lay out PV arrays on farmland \citep{zhang2026}. Machine learning for crop management has mainly used reinforcement learning with black-box simulators \citep{gautron2022}, which would need very many simulated seasons for global, multi-decadal assessments.

Two methodological strands are relevant. Differentiable process models, which couple physical simulators with learning, allow parameters and policies to be fitted by gradient descent through the simulator \citep{reichstein2019,shen2023}, and we use them for both calibration and control. Decision making under deep uncertainty asks for designs that perform acceptably across plausible models and not only under the best estimate \citep{marchau2019}, and scenario-based designs have finite-sample guarantees under convexity assumptions that we do not meet \citep{calafiore2006,campi2008}; our design rules are heuristic counterparts and we compare them with a simple safety margin.

Two questions follow. First, how much of the electricity that an agrivoltaic system can produce under a yield-retention floor comes from the array density, and how much from dynamic light-sharing control of the trackers? Second, how robust is that answer to the weakest part of any such study, the crop response to shade, and does a controller learned from a simulator add anything to a well-designed hand-written rule? Answering them requires a simulator that couples the physics of light, PV, microclimate and crop growth, a protocol in which all controllers face the same design constraints, a check of the shade response against field data, and enough sites and seasons to separate real effects from noise.

This paper makes three contributions. The first is a quantification of light-sharing control under a yield floor. Light sharing is parameterised by one daily level between full backtracking and the rotation that minimises the shadow on the crop, and we compare static arrays, a seasonal schedule, a rule that follows crop phenology, a rule that reacts to heat and drought stress, and a neural controller (SALS) trained through the simulator. The second is a deployable evaluation protocol. The array density of every site is chosen from its historical seasons only, with the same rule for every method, and performance is measured on held-out sites and years, over three training seeds and three spatial splits. The third is a differentiable agrivoltaic simulator that supports both, coupling hourly solar geometry, single-axis trackers with backtracking, row-to-row shading and PV electricity, the direct and diffuse light reaching the crop, canopy temperature and reference evapotranspiration under the modules, and the SIMPLE crop model \citep{zhao2019} extended with a flowering-stage shade penalty. All components are differentiable tensor operations \citep{karniadakis2021}. The PV part is benchmarked against PVGIS. The crop response to shade is learned from the open data of a meta-analysis \citep{laub2022}, checked against field trials on rice, wheat, potato and maize \citep{gonocruz2021,weselek2021,ramosfuentes2023}, and propagated through the simulator as an ensemble.

We apply the protocol to 400 crop--site pairs that represent where maize, wheat, rice, soybean and potato are grown worldwide, sampled in proportion to their harvested area \citep{yu2020} with local crop calendars \citep{jagermeyr2021}, and driven by NASA POWER daily weather for 2001--2019 \citep{sparks2018} under baseline and +1.5, +2 and +3\,\textdegree C climates. The main findings are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Light sharing is the main lever on electricity at a given floor, although economically it pays only at high prices (item 6). At the same mean yield retention of 90\%, arrays with light-sharing control carry 2.1 to 2.9 times the module area of static arrays and produce 1.8--2.4 times as much electricity (Section~\ref{sec:main}).
\item The best hand-written rule follows crop phenology and needs no weather forecast. Rules that react to heat or drought stress are not better and depend on forecast quality (Section~\ref{sec:extra}).
\item The crop response to shade can be learned from data. A hierarchical model fitted to 428 observations from 58 field studies predicts a held-out study of the four staple crop types to about 0.22 in relative yield, no better than proportional loss (it is better only for crop types the simulator does not represent), so between-study spread is large. The simulator is close to these curves for cereals and potato but milder than the meta-analysis for maize and soybean, whereas one maize trial is milder still; the gains for those two crops are therefore uncertain and possibly overestimated (Section~\ref{sec:mlresults}).
\item A learned controller adds a small gain over the best rule: 0.044 LER over the written phenology rule and 0.018 over a rule tuned on the same training seasons. Training it on ensembles of shade responses, or giving it a farm-measurable biomass signal, does not help, and with the wider ensembles it did not retain its margin over the written rule in our training budget (Section~\ref{sec:uncertain}).
\item The bottleneck is the uncertainty of the crop response that enters the design. A density chosen under the mean response leaves about 70\% of crop--site pairs at the floor when another plausible response holds (oracle 85--87\%). A rule built on the quantiles of an ensemble of responses raises this by 8 points for 0.03 LER, and a floor raised by 0.02 under the mean response gives no detectable difference at equal compliance (the intervals are too wide to establish equivalence); no rule is safe outside the ensemble, so the ensemble is useful to size a margin (Section~\ref{sec:uncertain}).
\item Under illustrative costs, light sharing breaks even with the open field at 70 to 92\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ (static arrays at 59), learning is worth about 2\,kUSD\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ over a tuned rule at 90\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$, and a conventional plant replacing the crop is more profitable above its break-even of 48\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ (Section~\ref{sec:cost}).
\item At the yield floor, agrivoltaics on 5\% of the world's staple cropland could supply about 14\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$, but only with about 10\,TWp of modules, several times the installed PV capacity of the world (Section~\ref{sec:global}).
\end{enumerate}
