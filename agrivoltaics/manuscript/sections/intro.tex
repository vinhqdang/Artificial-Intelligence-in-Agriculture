\section{Introduction}\label{sec:intro}

Agrivoltaic (AV) systems put crops and photovoltaic (PV) modules on the same land, so that a hectare can deliver food and electricity together \citep{dupraz2011,dinesh2016}. Land-equivalent ratios (LER) above one have been reported in field trials and models \citep{amaducci2018,valle2017,trommsdorff2021}, and partial shade can lower canopy temperature and conserve soil water \citep{marrou2013,adeh2018,weselek2021}. The shade also reduces the light available for photosynthesis, and regulators increasingly require AV systems to retain most of the reference yield; the German pre-standard DIN SPEC 91434 sets a floor of 66\% \citep{din2021}, and other schemes and farmers set stricter ones. Technology that lets an AV system follow such a floor, by choosing the density of the array and the rotation of its trackers, is therefore a problem of control and design for agriculture.

Single-axis trackers add a degree of freedom: modules can be rotated away from the sun to let light through when the crop needs it \citep{valle2017}, or towards the sun to maximise electricity. We call the daily choice between the two \emph{light sharing}. It is a sequential decision problem, because the value of light depends on the crop stage, soil water and weather, and the consequence appears at harvest. Learning-based control for crop management has mostly used reinforcement learning with black-box simulators \citep{gautron2022}, which would need very many simulated seasons for global multi-decadal assessments. Differentiable process models, which fit parameters and policies by gradient descent through a simulator \citep{reichstein2019,shen2023}, are an alternative that has not been applied to AV control. A second difficulty is that the crop response to shade, the weakest part of any AV model, is uncertain, which calls for designs that perform acceptably across plausible models and not only under the best estimate \citep{marchau2019}.

This paper develops and tests a differentiable-simulation approach to AV tracker control and design, and asks what learning adds. We built a differentiable simulator that couples hourly solar geometry, tracker and PV physics, light and microclimate under the modules, and the SIMPLE crop model \citep{zhao2019} extended with a flowering-stage shade penalty, and trained a neural controller (SALS) through whole seasons by back-propagation. We compared it with static arrays and with written and tuned rules in a protocol in which the array density of every site is chosen from its own history only and performance is measured on geographically held-out sites and years. The shade response was learned from the open data of a meta-analysis \citep{laub2022}, checked against field trials \citep{gonocruz2021,weselek2021,ramosfuentes2023} and propagated as an ensemble of plausible responses; on that ensemble we tested design margins calibrated by conformal risk control and a learned design network. The protocol was applied to 400 crop--site pairs sampled in proportion to harvested area \citep{yu2020}, with NASA POWER weather for 2001--2020 (19 sowing years) \citep{sparks2018} under baseline and +1.5, +2 and +3\,\textdegree C climates. The evaluation is by simulation on public data, without field experiments under trackers. The main findings are as follows.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Differentiable simulation makes gradient-based training of a tracker controller through whole seasons tractable (300 steps), but the learned controller adds 0.044 LER over a written phenology rule and only 0.007 over a window optimised on the training seasons, with no difference that can be distinguished from zero at matched retention.
\item Light sharing by rule is the main lever on electricity for single-axis trackers: arrays carry 2.1 to 2.9 times the module area of static arrays at the same mean retention of 90\%, at the price of missing the floor in 24--31\% of seasons.
\item The shade response is only partly learnable: a hierarchical model fitted to 428 observations predicts a new study to about 0.22 in relative yield, no better than proportional loss for the four staple crop types, and the simulator is milder than the data for maize.
\item A density chosen under the mean response leaves 70\% of pairs at the floor when another plausible response holds. A conformal margin attains a stated compliance inside the ensemble, a learned design network does worse than a simple margin, and only large margins are safe outside the ensemble.
\end{enumerate}
