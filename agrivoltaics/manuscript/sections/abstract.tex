\begin{abstract}
Single-axis trackers can shift their daily rotation from maximum electricity towards letting light through (light sharing). We quantify its value under a 90\% yield-retention floor with a differentiable simulator. On 92 held-out crop--site pairs and the proportional-growth shade response, light-sharing rules allowed arrays 2.1--2.9 times as dense as static arrays at equal mean retention and raised the land-equivalent ratio (LER) from 1.07 to 1.18--1.24, with the floor met in 69--76\% of seasons (static arrays: 93\%). The crop response to shade is the least certain input: a hierarchical model fitted to 428 observations from 58 studies predicts a new study of the four staple crop types (138 observations, 30 studies) only to about 0.22 in relative yield, no better than proportional loss, so we built ensembles of plausible responses from the learned curves and field trials. Within these ensembles, a density chosen under the mean response left about 70\% of pairs at the floor when another plausible response held, against 85--87\% for an oracle design with the true response; a floor raised by 0.02 and an ensemble-quantile rule were not distinguishable at equal compliance, and neither was safe outside the ensemble, whose spread is narrower than the between-study spread. Pooled over three splits (76 pairs), a neural controller initialised from the written phenology rule beat it by 0.044 LER but a CMA-ES-optimised window, re-tuned on each split, by only 0.007 (95\% interval 0.002--0.014; 0.003 at matched retention, interval including zero); ensemble or biomass-signal training did not help within our budget. Under illustrative costs, light sharing breaks even with the open field at floor-designed densities at 70--92\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ (static arrays at 59) and a conventional plant is more profitable above 48. For maize and soybean (53\% of the upscaled 14\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$) the simulator's bias is uncertain.
\end{abstract}
