\begin{abstract}
Single-axis trackers can shift their daily rotation from maximum electricity towards letting light through (light sharing). We quantify its value under a 90\% yield-retention floor with a differentiable simulator. On 92 held-out crop--site pairs and the proportional-growth shade response, light-sharing rules allowed arrays 2.1--2.9 times as dense as static arrays at equal mean retention and raised the land-equivalent ratio (LER) from 1.07 to 1.18--1.24; the floor was met in 69--76\% of seasons (static arrays: 93\%). The crop response to shade is the least certain input: a hierarchical model fitted to 428 observations from 58 studies predicts a new study of the four staple crop types to about 0.22 in relative yield, no better than proportional loss, so we built ensembles of plausible responses. Within them, a density chosen under the mean response left about 70\% of pairs at the floor when another plausible response held, against 85--87\% for an oracle design; a raised floor and an ensemble-quantile rule were not distinguishable at equal compliance, and neither was safe outside the ensemble, which is mostly narrower than the between-study spread. Pooled over three splits (76 pairs), a neural controller exceeded the written phenology rule by 0.044 LER but a CMA-ES-optimised window, re-tuned on each split, by only 0.007 (95\% interval 0.002--0.014); at matched retention the margin was 0.003 and not distinguishable from zero. Ensemble or biomass-signal training did not help. Under illustrative costs, light sharing breaks even with the open field at 70--92\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ at floor-designed densities (static arrays 59), and a conventional plant, assumed 25\% cheaper per watt, is more profitable above 48 (63 at equal capex). The simulator's bias for maize is uncertain.
\end{abstract}
