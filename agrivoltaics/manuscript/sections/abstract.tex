\begin{abstract}
Single-axis trackers can shift their daily rotation from maximum electricity towards letting light through (light sharing). We quantify its value under a 90\% yield-retention floor with a differentiable simulator. On 92 held-out crop--site pairs and the proportional-growth shade response, light-sharing rules allowed arrays 2.1--2.9 times as dense as static arrays and raised the land-equivalent ratio (LER) from 1.07 to 1.18--1.24. The crop response to shade is the least certain input: a model fitted to 428 observations predicts a new study of the four staple crop types to about 0.22 in relative yield, no better than proportional loss, so we built ensembles of plausible responses. A density chosen under the mean response left about 70\% of pairs at the floor when another plausible response held, against 85--87\% for an oracle design; a raised floor and an ensemble-quantile rule were not distinguishable at equal compliance, and neither was safe outside the ensemble. Pooled over three splits (76 pairs), a neural controller exceeded the written phenology rule by 0.044 LER but a CMA-ES-optimised window, re-tuned on each split, by only 0.007 (95\% interval 0.002--0.014; 0.003 at matched retention, not distinguishable from zero). Under illustrative costs, light sharing breaks even with the open field at 70--92\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ (static arrays 59), and a conventional plant replacing the crop, assumed 25\% cheaper per watt, is more profitable than every agrivoltaic option above 48 (about 64 at equal capex). The simulator's bias for maize is uncertain.
\end{abstract}
