\begin{abstract}
Agrivoltaic systems grow crops under photovoltaic (PV) modules, but the electricity they can produce without compromising food production is usually set by a fixed array geometry. Single-axis trackers offer another lever: the daily rotation can be shifted from maximum electricity towards letting light through, a choice we call light sharing. We quantify how much this lever is worth under a food-security floor (90\% of the open-field yield) and how robust the answer is. We built a differentiable simulator that couples solar geometry, trackers with backtracking, PV output, crop light and microclimate, a soil-water balance and the SIMPLE crop model with a flowering-stage shade penalty. The PV part agrees with PVGIS at 29 sites ($r=0.97$, mean bias $-4.9$\%). Because the crop response to shade is the least certain part, we fitted it by gradient descent through the simulator to a meta-analysis and four field trials, obtaining an ensemble of plausible responses; the proportional response used in the main analysis is conservative against the trials, so the reported gains are lower bounds. All methods were compared in a deployable protocol: the array density of every site was chosen from its historical seasons with one rule, and performance was measured on geographically held-out sites and years, for 400 crop--site pairs representing the harvested area of wheat, rice, maize, soybean and potato, in baseline and +1.5, +2 and +3\,\textdegree C climates. At equal mean yield retention (about 0.905), simple light-sharing rules allowed arrays about three times as dense as static arrays and raised the land-equivalent ratio (LER) from 1.07 to 1.18--1.24 and electricity from 217 to 387--465\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$. A rule that follows crop phenology, which needs no weather forecast, was the best hand-written rule, and its gain over static arrays rose from 0.17 to 0.19 and 0.30 LER under calibrated and field-fitted responses. A neural controller trained through the simulator added only 0.01--0.05 LER; training it on the ensemble of responses, or giving it a farm-measurable biomass signal, changed nothing, and a feedback rule on that signal was worse than the phenology rule. The decision that mattered was the design: choosing the density under the ensemble mean met the floor at 55--58\% of sites when the true response was another plausible one, against 74--76\% with an oracle, and choosing it so that the floor holds in 75\% of plausible responses restored the oracle level for 0.02--0.03 LER. The floor was met in 70\% of individual seasons, and production-weighted retention was below 0.9 for soybean and maize, so per-site enforcement needs region- and crop-specific design. Equipping 5\% of the world's staple cropland at the floor would yield about 14\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ but would need about 10\,TWp of modules, 5.7 times the installed global PV capacity.
\end{abstract}
