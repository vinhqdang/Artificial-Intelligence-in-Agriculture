\begin{abstract}
Single-axis trackers let agrivoltaic arrays shift their daily rotation from maximum electricity towards letting light through (light sharing). We quantify its value under a yield-retention floor of 90\% with a differentiable simulator of the whole system. The crop response to shade is the least certain part: a hierarchical model fitted to 428 observations from 58 field studies predicts a new study of the four staple crop types only to about 0.22 in relative yield, no better than proportional loss, so we turned the learned curves and four field trials into ensembles of plausible responses by gradient descent through the simulator. With the proportional-growth shade response, on 92 held-out crop--site pairs, light-sharing rules allowed arrays 2.1--2.9 times as dense as static arrays at equal retention and raised the land-equivalent ratio (LER) from 1.07 to 1.18--1.24 (1.27 for a neural controller); the floor was met in 69--76\% of seasons. Pooled over three splits, the neural controller beat the written phenology rule by 0.044 LER, a tuned rule by 0.018 (95\% interval 0.012--0.024) and a CMA-ES-optimised schedule by 0.013; training on ensembles or a biomass signal did not help within our budget (one seed, 21 pairs). The design step carried more compliance risk than control (pairs at the floor: 70\% to 88\% across design rules, 87\% to 91\% across light-sharing controllers): a density chosen under the mean response left about 70\% when another plausible response held (oracle 85--87\%); a floor raised by 0.02 and an ensemble-quantile rule gave no detectable difference at equal compliance; neither was safe outside the ensemble. Under illustrative costs, light sharing breaks even with the open field at 70--92\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ (static arrays at 59) and a conventional plant is more profitable above 48. The simulator's bias is uncertain for maize and soybean (53\% of the upscaled 14\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$).
\end{abstract}
