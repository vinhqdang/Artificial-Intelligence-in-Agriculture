\begin{abstract}
Agrivoltaic systems grow crops under photovoltaic (PV) modules, but the electricity they can produce without compromising food production is usually set by a fixed array geometry. Single-axis trackers offer another lever: the daily rotation can be shifted from maximum electricity towards letting light through, a choice we call light sharing. We quantify how much this lever is worth under a food-security floor (90\% of the open-field yield) and how robust the answer is. We built a differentiable simulator that couples solar geometry, trackers with backtracking, PV output, crop light and microclimate, a soil-water balance and the SIMPLE crop model with a flowering-stage shade penalty. The PV part agrees with PVGIS at 29 sites ($r=0.97$, mean bias $-4.9$\%). The crop response to shade was compared with a meta-analysis and with field trials of rice, wheat, potato and maize. The simulated response is close to proportional and is conservative against the trials, which makes the reported gains lower bounds. All methods were compared in a deployable protocol: the array density of every site was chosen from its historical seasons with one rule, and performance was measured on geographically held-out sites and years, for 400 crop--site pairs representing the harvested area of wheat, rice, maize, soybean and potato, in baseline and +1.5, +2 and +3\,\textdegree C climates. At equal mean yield retention (about 0.905), simple light-sharing rules allowed arrays about three times as dense as static arrays and raised the land-equivalent ratio (LER) from 1.07 to 1.18--1.24 and electricity from 217 to 387--465\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$. A rule that follows crop phenology, which needs no weather forecast, was the best hand-written rule. It remained best, and its gain over static arrays rose from 0.17 to 0.19 and 0.30 LER when the shade response was calibrated or fitted to the field trials. A neural controller trained through the simulator added a smaller gain (0.03--0.05 LER, 84--86\% of held-out sites), consistently over seeds, splits, canopy-cooling assumptions and climates, but it learned a smooth function of phenology rather than a reaction to heat or drought. The floor was met in 70\% of individual seasons, and production-weighted retention was below 0.9 for soybean and maize, so per-site enforcement needs region- and crop-specific design. Equipping 5\% of the world's staple cropland at the floor would yield about 14\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ but would need about 10\,TWp of modules, 5.7 times the installed global PV capacity.
\end{abstract}
