\begin{abstract}
Agrivoltaic systems grow crops under photovoltaic (PV) modules, but the electricity they can produce without compromising food production is usually set by a fixed array geometry. Single-axis trackers add a lever: the daily rotation can be shifted from maximum electricity towards letting light through (light sharing). We quantify its value under a food-security floor of 90\% of open-field yield. A differentiable simulator couples solar geometry, trackers, PV output (PVGIS benchmark: $r=0.97$, bias $-4.9$\%), crop light, microclimate, soil water and the SIMPLE crop model. Because the crop response to shade is the least certain part, we fitted it by gradient descent through the simulator to a meta-analysis and four field trials, obtaining an ensemble of plausible responses. In a deployable protocol (density chosen from each site's history, held-out sites and years, 400 crop--site pairs, baseline to +3\,\textdegree C), light-sharing rules allowed arrays three times as dense as static arrays at equal retention (0.905) and raised the land-equivalent ratio (LER) from 1.07 to 1.18--1.24. A phenology rule needing no forecast was the best rule, and its gain grew under calibrated responses. A neural controller trained through the simulator added only 0.01--0.05 LER; training on the ensemble or adding a farm-measurable biomass signal changed nothing. The decision that mattered was the design: a density chosen under the mean response met the floor at 55--58\% of sites when another plausible response held, against 74--76\% for an oracle, and a density meeting the floor in 75\% of plausible responses restored the oracle level for 0.02--0.03 LER. Staple cropland equipped at 5\% would give about 14\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ but needs about 10\,TWp of modules.
\end{abstract}
