\begin{abstract}
Single-axis trackers let agrivoltaic arrays shift the daily rotation from maximum electricity towards letting light through (light sharing). We quantify its value under a food-security floor of 90\% of open-field yield. A differentiable simulator couples solar geometry, trackers, PV output (PVGIS benchmark: $r=0.97$), crop light, microclimate, soil water and the SIMPLE crop model. The crop response to shade is its least certain part, so we learned it from data: a hierarchical model fitted to 428 observations from 58 field studies predicted held-out studies with a 28\% lower error than proportional loss (RMSE 0.31 against 0.43), and gradient descent through the simulator turned it into an ensemble of plausible responses. In a deployable protocol (density chosen from each site's history, held-out sites and years, 400 crop--site pairs, baseline to +3\,\textdegree C), light-sharing rules allowed arrays three times as dense as static arrays at equal retention and raised the land-equivalent ratio (LER) from 1.07 to 1.18--1.24; a phenology rule needing no forecast was the best. A neural controller trained through the simulator added only 0.01--0.05 LER, and neither training on the ensemble nor a farm-measurable biomass signal changed this. The decision that mattered was the design: a density chosen under the mean response met the floor at 62--63\% of sites when another plausible response held (oracle 73--79\%), and a density meeting the floor in 75\% of plausible responses raised this to 71--73\% for 0.04 LER. The simulator is too mild for maize and soybean, so gains for these crops are probably overestimated. Equipping 5\% of staple cropland would give about 14\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ but needs about 10\,TWp of modules.
\end{abstract}
