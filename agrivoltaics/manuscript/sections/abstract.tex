\begin{abstract}
Single-axis trackers let agrivoltaic arrays shift the daily rotation from maximum electricity towards letting light through (light sharing). We quantify its value under a food-security floor of 90\% of open-field yield with a differentiable simulator of solar geometry, trackers, PV output, crop light, microclimate, soil water and the SIMPLE crop model. The crop response to shade is the least certain part. A hierarchical model fitted to 428 observations from 58 field studies predicts a new study of the four staple crop types only to about 0.22 in relative yield, no better than proportional loss, and we turned the learned curves and four field trials into an ensemble of plausible responses by gradient descent through the simulator. In a deployable protocol (density chosen from each site's history, held-out sites and years, 400 crop--site pairs), light-sharing rules allowed arrays three times as dense as static arrays at equal retention and raised the land-equivalent ratio (LER) from 1.07 to 1.18--1.24; a phenology rule needing no forecast was the best. A neural controller trained through the simulator exceeded the written rule by 0.044 LER but a rule tuned on the same training seasons by only 0.018 (95\% interval 0.012--0.024); training on the ensemble or adding a farm-measurable biomass signal changed nothing. The design decision mattered more: a density chosen under the mean response left about 70\% of crop--site pairs at the floor when another plausible response held (oracle 85--87\%), and a floor raised by 0.02 under the mean response did better at equal compliance than a rule built on the ensemble's quantiles. The simulator is too mild for maize and soybean, so gains for these crops are probably overestimated. Equipping 5\% of staple cropland would give about 14\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ but needs about 10\,TWp of modules.
\end{abstract}
