\begin{abstract}
Agrivoltaic systems grow crops under photovoltaic (PV) modules and could relieve the competition between food and solar power for land. How much electricity they can produce without compromising food production depends on how sunlight is shared between modules and plants. We built a differentiable agrivoltaic simulator that couples solar geometry, single-axis trackers with backtracking, PV output, crop light and microclimate, a soil-water balance and the SIMPLE crop model with a flowering-stage shade penalty. The PV part agrees with PVGIS at 29 sites ($r=0.97$, mean bias $-4.9$\%). We trained a neural controller (SALS), initialised from a phenology-based rule and fine-tuned by back-propagation through whole seasons, to set the daily light-sharing level of the trackers, maximising electricity under a 90\% yield-retention floor. In a deployable evaluation, the array density of every site was chosen from its historical seasons with the same rule for all controllers. Performance was measured on geographically held-out sites and years, for 400 crop--site pairs representing the harvested area of wheat, rice, maize, soybean and potato, in baseline and +1.5, +2 and +3\,\textdegree C climates. At equal mean yield retention (about 0.905), light-sharing control allowed arrays about three times as dense as static arrays. Electricity rose from 217 to 510\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$, and the land-equivalent ratio (LER) from 1.07 to 1.27. The learned controller exceeded the best hand-written rule (phenology rule, LER 1.24) by 0.03--0.05 in LER (about 10\% more electricity), at 84--86\% of the held-out sites, consistently over three seeds, three spatial splits, three canopy-cooling assumptions and all climates. Its advantage did not depend on stress-aware inputs, on training in warmed climates or on weather forecasts, and it held for floors between 0.80 and 0.95: the learned policy is a smooth function of crop phenology. The floor was met in 70\% of individual seasons and production-weighted retention was below 0.9 for soybean and maize, so per-site enforcement requires region- and crop-specific design. Equipping 5\% of the world's staple cropland at the floor would yield about 14\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$, about half of world generation, but it would need about 10\,TWp of modules, 5.7 times the installed global PV capacity. The results quantify how far dynamic control extends agrivoltaics and where the remaining benefit of learning lies.
\end{abstract}
