\begin{abstract}
Single-axis trackers let agrivoltaic arrays shift the daily rotation from maximum electricity towards letting light through (light sharing). We quantify its value under a food-security floor of 90\% of open-field yield. A differentiable simulator couples solar geometry, trackers, PV output, crop light, microclimate, soil water and the SIMPLE crop model. The crop response to shade is its least certain part, so we learned it from data: a hierarchical model fitted to 428 observations from 58 field studies predicts a new study of the four staple crop types only to about 0.22 in relative yield, no better than proportional loss, so between-study spread is large; gradient descent through the simulator turned the learned curves and four field trials into an ensemble of plausible responses. In a deployable protocol (density chosen from each site's history, held-out sites and years, 400 crop--site pairs), light-sharing rules allowed arrays three times as dense as static arrays at equal retention and raised the land-equivalent ratio (LER) from 1.07 to 1.18--1.24; a phenology rule needing no forecast was the best. A neural controller trained through the simulator added only 0.01--0.05 LER, and neither training on the ensemble nor a farm-measurable biomass signal changed this. The decision that mattered was the design: a density chosen under the mean response left 67--70\% of sites at the floor when another plausible response held (oracle 83--87\%), and a density meeting the floor in 75\% of plausible responses raised this to 77--78\% (+8 to +10 points, 95\% intervals 5--14) for 0.04 LER, about half of the gap to the oracle. The simulator is too mild for maize and soybean, so gains for these crops are probably overestimated. Equipping 5\% of staple cropland would give about 14\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ but needs about 10\,TWp of modules.
\end{abstract}
