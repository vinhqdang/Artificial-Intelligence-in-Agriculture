\begin{abstract}
Single-axis trackers let agrivoltaic arrays shift the daily rotation from maximum electricity towards letting light through (light sharing). We quantify its value under a yield-retention floor of 90\% of open-field yield with a differentiable simulator of the whole system. The crop response to shade is the least certain part: a hierarchical model fitted to 428 observations from 58 field studies predicts a new study of the four staple crop types only to about 0.22 in relative yield, no better than proportional loss, so we turned the learned curves and four field trials into ensembles of plausible responses by gradient descent through the simulator. On the 92 held-out crop--site pairs of the main split, light-sharing rules allowed arrays 2.1--2.9 times as dense as static arrays at equal mean retention and raised the land-equivalent ratio (LER) from 1.07 to 1.18--1.24 (1.27 for a neural controller), with a phenology rule needing no forecast the best; the floor was met in 69--76\% of seasons. Pooled over three splits (76 pairs), the neural controller exceeded the written rule by 0.044 LER and a rule tuned on the same seasons by 0.018 (95\% interval 0.012--0.024); a CMA-ES-optimised schedule was 0.013 behind it, and training on ensembles or a farm-measurable biomass signal did not help. Design mattered more: a density chosen under the mean response left about 70\% of pairs at the floor when another plausible response held (oracle 85--87\%); a floor raised by 0.02 (85--88\% of pairs, 0.06--0.07 LER below the mean-response design) was as good as or slightly better than an ensemble-quantile rule, and none of these rules was safe outside the ensemble (7--33\% of pairs under three fixed responses). Under illustrative costs, light sharing breaks even with the open field at 70--92\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ and static arrays at 59, and a conventional plant is more profitable above 48. For maize and soybean (53\% of the upscaled 14\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$) the bias of the simulator is uncertain.
\end{abstract}
