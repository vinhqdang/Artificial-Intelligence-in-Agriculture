\section{Results}\label{sec:results}
Further analyses are in the Supplementary Material (Section~\ref{S-sec:supp-results}): other warming levels, floors, canopy cooling, forecast errors, layouts, controllers trained on ensembles of responses, and the cross-validation tables of the shade-response model.

\subsection{Light sharing under a yield floor}\label{sec:r-main}
Table~\ref{tab:m-main} compares the controllers on the 92 held-out crop--site pairs of the main split, after the density of each site has been chosen from its 2001--2014 seasons with the same rule for all controllers; all methods therefore keep the mean retention close to 90\%, and differences in electricity are differences in the density that each controller can sustain. Without light sharing the floor limits ground coverage to 6\% and arrays produce 217\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ (LER 1.07). Sharing light during the cropping season allows 17\% coverage and 397\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ (LER 1.19), and a written rule that follows crop phenology reaches 465 (LER 1.24). A stress rule that shades the crop on forecast heat or drought days is worse (LER 1.18), because it cannot sustain the density. SALS sustains 17\% coverage, produces 510\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ and reaches an LER of 1.270; this is $+0.038$ over the phenology rule (site-mean contrast, 95\% interval 0.025--0.053, higher at 84\% of the 92 sites; the season-pooled values of Table~\ref{tab:m-main} give 1.270 against 1.236) and $+0.205$ (0.176--0.236) over static arrays, with 2.35 times the electricity at 2.9 times the density (Fig.~\ref{fig:m-frontier}). The ranking, SALS ahead of the phenology rule, ahead of the stress rule and seasonal sharing, ahead of static arrays, holds in every warming scenario (SALS LER 1.30 at $+3$\,\textdegree C), for floors of 0.80 to 0.95 (SALS minus phenology rule 0.033--0.037 LER), for canopy cooling of 0, 1 and 2\,\textdegree C (0.029, 0.034, 0.042), under larger forecast errors and under alternative layouts (Supplement). Masking the soil-water, aridity and forecast inputs of SALS leaves its LER unchanged (1.269 against 1.270). The price of light sharing is compliance: the floor is met in 69--76\% of seasons, against 93\% for static arrays, which also keep a slightly higher mean retention (0.917 against about 0.905) because the density grid forces a coarser choice at low density.

Benefits differ between crops: soybean and maize tolerate the densest arrays (GCR 0.24 and 0.19; LER 1.41 and 1.30), potato follows (0.19; 1.31), and rice and wheat are the most restrictive (0.13 and 0.15; LER 1.20 for both). The floor is not met evenly. Production-weighted retention is 0.888 for soybean and 0.898 for maize because high-yielding seasons lose more, and regionally it is lowest in Latin America and Oceania (0.862, 95\% site-bootstrap interval 0.841--0.897, four sites, GCR 0.41) and Africa and the Middle East (0.894, 0.868--0.908, eight sites), against 0.902 for South Asia (39 sites). Only the first deficit lies outside the interval of South Asia, so the regional comparison is an indication and not a finding.

\subsection{What learning adds}\label{sec:r-learning}
The written phenology rule was not tuned, whereas SALS is trained, so we tuned its window on the training seasons of each split, by a grid search and by CMA-ES over a five-parameter schedule. Pooled over three splits and 76 distinct pairs (SALS: mean of three training seeds per split), tuning the window raises LER over the written rule by 0.026 (grid; 95\% cluster-bootstrap interval 0.016--0.037) and 0.036 (CMA-ES; 0.023--0.052), and SALS by 0.044 (0.030--0.060) (Table~\ref{tab:m-ctl}). Optimising a window therefore accounts for about 83\% of the margin of SALS over the written rule. The optimum is an almost binary window from 0.20 to 0.93 of the season, so a smooth ramp is not needed to reproduce most of what SALS does. SALS exceeds the CMA-ES window by 0.007 LER (0.002--0.014), or 2.4\% more electricity at the designed densities; the margin is positive on each split (0.008, 0.009 and 0.005, with the interval of the third including zero) and for each of the three training seeds (0.007, 0.008, 0.007). The design step quantises density to a grid of 0.05, much coarser than this margin, so we also compared the controllers at matched retention, reading LER at a retention of 0.90 off each pair's retention--LER curve: SALS exceeds the CMA-ES window by 0.003 (95\% interval $-0.0004$ to 0.007; 73 pairs whose retention curves bracket 0.90), which is not distinguishable from zero. The source of the remaining margin is not identified: it does not come from soil water, forecasts or aridity, and canopy development and density remain untested. Training SALS on ensembles of shade responses or giving it a farm-measurable biomass signal did not help within our budget (Supplement), and SALS-robust was below SALS even under responses inside its training ensemble, which may point to under-training.

\subsection{Validation and the shade response}\label{sec:m-valid}
\emph{PV model.} The simulated specific yield of an isolated row agrees with PVGIS at 29 sites ($r=0.971$, simulator 4.9\% lower). At higher ground coverage we compared plane-of-array irradiance with pvlib \citep{holmgren2018} (infinite sheds, isotropic sky, backtracking, same hourly irradiance, 12 sites): the simulator is 0.4\% above pvlib at $g=0.05$ and 0.7, 1.4, 2.1, 2.6 and 3.0\% above at $g=0.1$ to 0.5, so it underestimates the loss relative to a sparse array (2.8\% instead of 4.4\% at $g=0.3$). Correcting the electricity of the arrays and of the reference plant by this ratio raises the LER of light-sharing methods by 0.003--0.004 (0.008 with the squared ratio), leaves the margins of SALS unchanged within 0.0003, raises break-even prices by about 0.8\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ and changes no ranking. The comparison is model-to-model and does not test the ground-side light.

\emph{Shade response.} Fig.~\ref{fig:m-datashade} shows the observations of the meta-analysis \citep{laub2022}, the learned curves and the simulator. In leave-one-study-out cross-validation over all 339 shaded observations a hierarchical crop-type model reduces the error from 0.43 (proportional loss) to 0.31, but this gain comes from crop types that the simulator does not represent. For the four staple crop types the learned curves are no better than proportional loss (0.222 against 0.220; difference $+0.002$, interval $-0.023$ to $+0.030$), and gradient-boosted trees are worse. The error of 0.22 is the between-study spread that a design has to cover. The simulator is close to the data for cereals and potato, and conservative against the field trials of rice, wheat and potato (for example rice 0.79 against 0.84 \citep{gonocruz2021}), but milder than the meta-analysis for maize (0.75 against 0.42 at 40\% shading); for soybean its value (0.48) lies within the interval of the data (0.40, 80\% interval 0.34--0.53). One maize trial reports smaller losses still \citep{sekiyama2019}, so for maize the direction of the bias is uncertain.

\subsection{Design under shade-response uncertainty}\label{sec:m-design}
Table~\ref{tab:learndesign} and Fig.~\ref{fig:m-learn} use ensemble E2 (five members that differ by 0.02 to 0.06 in relative yield, against the between-study error of 0.22). The truth is a held-out member and the design belief is the mean of the other members; all numbers are for 76 distinct pairs, with a pair held out in several splits averaged. A density chosen under the mean response leaves 70\% of pairs at the floor, against 87\% for an oracle design under the true response; the oracle's LER (1.486) is slightly below that of the mean-response design (1.493), which obtains it by missing the floor more often. Raising the floor used in the design to 0.92 gives 88\% at 0.067 less LER, 0.94 gives 97\% and 0.96 gives 99\%, and an ensemble-quantile rule lies on the same frontier as the margin (Supplement), so the ensemble is useful to size a margin and not to replace it. Compliance by crop is uneven (54--80\% under the mean response, 80--98\% for a margin of 0.92).

\emph{Conformal margins.} Conformal risk control \citep{angelopoulos2024} picks the smallest margin whose adjusted estimate of the average share of members at which a pair misses the floor is at most a target, which bounds the expected share of misses for a new pair if pairs are exchangeable. We calibrated it on the pairs of two splits that are not in the third split (margins of 0.915--0.930) and evaluated it on the third. The pooled compliance of the test pairs was 94, 89 and 86\% for targets of 90, 85 and 80\%, and 82.5--97.1\% across splits; the largest shortfall below a target was 2.5 points (split 0, target 85\%), within the sampling error of about 5 points for 26--35 pairs per split. The guarantee concerns the average over members, not each member, and it is not available per crop, because there are only 3 to 9 calibration pairs per crop and split: a single margin gives 78--100\% by crop, and we did not find evidence that per-crop margins help.

\emph{Learned design.} A neural network (one hidden layer of 32 units) that chooses the density from the retention curves of the other members (mean, minimum and maximum at each density), the crop and the latitude, trained for each penalty $\lambda$ to minimise $-\mathrm{LER}+\lambda\,\mathbf{1}[r<0.9]$ on the pairs of two splits (three seeds; test-split pairs excluded), does not improve on the margin: at matched compliance its LER is lower than that of the margin frontier by 0.02--0.06 for compliance between 75 and 91\% (for example $-0.057$, 95\% interval $-0.076$ to $-0.032$, at 87\%), and the difference is not distinguishable from zero above 96\%. The comparison favours the margins, whose single parameter is read off the test results, but the network has no advantage that this could hide.

\emph{Outside the ensemble.} The truth was also one of four fixed responses (proportional, calibrated to the meta-analysis, field-fitted and harsh), with the design belief the mean of the 12 members of E2; only the field-fitted response lies within the range spanned by the ensemble, and the pooled values average the four, so a value of 25\% for a crop means that one of four responses is met. No rule is safe: conformal margins reach 32--37\%, the network 35--43\%, a margin of 0.96 73\% at a cost of about 0.2 in LER relative to the mean-response design, and the oracle 88\%. Wheat, rice and potato pairs are at the floor in 3--29\% of cases for the mean-response design, the margin of 0.92 and the conformal margins, whereas soybean pairs meet it in 90--100\%, because the ensemble already contains steep soybean responses (Supplement). The guarantees hold only within the range of responses that the ensemble spans.

\subsection{Economics}\label{sec:m-cost}
All figures follow from assumed parameters (Supplementary Table~\ref{S-tab:costparams}) and are a sensitivity analysis, not a forecast. The economics are dominated by the installed capacity (annuity and operating cost of 11--32\,kUSD\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$) and not by the crop (revenue lost 0.3\,kUSD), so a method is worth what it earns per installed watt (Table~\ref{tab:m-cost}). With floor-designed densities, static arrays break even with the open field at 59\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ (95\% interval 56--61), whereas the light-sharing methods lose money at 60 (by 3 to 10\,kUSD) and break even at 70 (stress rule), 73 (SALS, 69--76; CMA-ES window, 70--77), 77 (grid-tuned window), 80 (written rule) and 92 (seasonal sharing). At 90\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ static arrays (6.3\,kUSD) are better than the written rule (4.0), close to the CMA-ES window (7.3) and worse than SALS (7.6); the difference between SALS and static arrays is $+1.3$ (95\% interval $-0.04$ to 2.7) and the one between SALS and the CMA-ES window 0.3 ($-0.1$ to 0.8), neither distinguishable from zero. If each site chooses the density that maximises its historical net value, SALS overtakes static arrays at about 75\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ and the CMA-ES window at about 78. A conventional plant that replaces the crop breaks even at 48 (46--50) and is more profitable than every agrivoltaic option above that price; it overtakes SALS above about 35 and static arrays above about 46. These plant results rest on an assumed capex of 0.75\,USD\,Wp$^{-1}$ against 1.0 for the elevated agrivoltaic structure, consistent with a levelised cost of electricity that is 38\% higher for agrophotovoltaics in a techno-economic study \citep{schindele2020}; with equal capex the plant breaks even at about 63 and overtakes SALS above about 58 and static arrays above about 64.

\subsection{Warming, regions and global potential}\label{sec:m-global}
Under $+3$\,\textdegree C the open-field production of the held-out sites falls by 23\% for maize and 18\% for rice, is nearly unchanged for soybean, and rises for wheat (3\%) and potato (10\%). Agrivoltaic production with light sharing follows the open field at about 90\% of its level and inherits the same impacts; in this model agrivoltaics does not protect staple production against warming. The LER of light-sharing systems, which is relative to the open field of the same climate, rises slightly with warming (SALS 1.270 to 1.297) because the floor allows slightly denser arrays. Scaled to the physical area of the five crops, agrivoltaics on 5\% of the world's staple cropland would supply about 14\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ at the yield floor, a scaling convention without an interval, which is about half of present world electricity generation and needs about 10.6\,TWp of modules at a capacity factor of 15.5\%, or about 5.6 times the global PV capacity of 2024 \citep{irena2025}; the figure is a technical potential, not a projection, and about half of it comes from maize and soybean, the crops with the least certain response. Ranking sites by electricity per hectare, the best 2\% of the cropland would supply about 20\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ and the best 4.7\% about 40; this is not a siting scenario and excludes grid, curtailment and land access. The best sites are mostly rainfed maize and soybean sites in the Americas and in southern and eastern Africa, where compliance is lowest.
