\subsection{Electricity and food on held-out sites and years}\label{sec:main}
Table~\ref{tab:main} compares the four controllers on the 92 held-out crop--site pairs of the main split (95 pairs are held out; pairs without a season whose open-field yield is at least 0.2 t\,ha$^{-1}$ in all four climates are excluded) (seasons sown in 2015--2019), after each site's density has been chosen from its 2001--2014 seasons with the same rule for all controllers. By construction all methods keep the mean yield retention close to 90\% (0.904--0.917), so differences in electricity are differences in the density that each controller can sustain under this floor.

\textbf{Static arrays are the weakest.} Without light sharing, the floor limits the ground coverage to 6\% on average, and arrays produce 217\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$, 16\% of a conventional PV plant per hectare (LER 1.07). Rotating the modules to let light through during the cropping season (seasonal sharing) allows a coverage of 17\% and 397\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ (LER 1.19). A rule that shares light only while the crop is growing (phenology rule) reaches 465\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ (LER 1.24). An expert rule that additionally shades the crop on forecast heat or drought days (stress rule) is worse than the phenology rule (LER 1.18): its density is limited to 13\% because it reduces light at times when the floor is most binding.

\textbf{The learned controller is best by a modest, consistent margin.} SALS sustains a density of 17\% (similar to the phenology rule), produces 510\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ and reaches an LER of 1.270 at the same retention. Compared with the phenology rule this is +0.038 in LER (95\% CI over sites 0.025--0.053; +10\% electricity) and it is higher at 84\% of the 92 sites (Table~\ref{S-tab:stats}). Compared with static arrays it is +0.205 (0.176--0.236), with 2.35 times the electricity at 2.9 times the density. The frontier in Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier} shows the same picture at matched densities: the learned policy lies on or slightly above the phenology rule over the whole density range, both lie well above seasonal sharing and the stress rule, and static arrays are far below.

\textbf{What the floor does and does not guarantee.} Enforcing the floor on the historical mean does not guarantee it in every season. In the test seasons, 70\% of SALS seasons have $r\ge0.9$ (phenology rule 69\%, stress rule 76\%, static arrays 93\%), and 2.7\% have $r<0.8$ (1.3\%, 0.9\% and 0.9\%). The lower tail of SALS is therefore slightly heavier than that of the rules: its 10th percentile of $r$ is 0.883, against 0.887, 0.886 and 0.903. Requiring $r\ge0.9$ in at least 80\% of a site's historical seasons (chance-constrained design) raises the season-level compliance to 90--94\% for the light-sharing controllers. It costs about 0.065 in LER for SALS (1.205), which remains the best controller (phenology rule 1.192; Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

\subsection{Robustness}\label{sec:sensitivity}
\textbf{Seeds and splits.} Three training seeds of SALS give LERs of 1.270, 1.268 and 1.269 under the baseline climate and 1.297, 1.298 and 1.297 at +3\,\textdegree C. On two further spatial splits, in which different blocks of sites are held out (training seed 0; the pooled analysis of Table~\ref{tab:ctl} uses three seeds per split), SALS exceeds the phenology rule by 0.047 and 0.041 in LER under the baseline climate and by 0.052 and 0.039 at +3\,\textdegree C (main split, season-pooled: 0.034 and 0.032; the site-level paired contrasts of Table~\ref{S-tab:stats} are 0.038 and 0.034). In every split SALS ranks first, the phenology rule second and static arrays last.

\textbf{Canopy cooling.} The canopy-cooling coefficient $\kappa$ is the most uncertain microclimate parameter. We repeated the whole protocol, including the historical design step, with $\kappa=0$, 1 and 2\,\textdegree C. Electricity changes with $\kappa$ because cooler canopies tolerate denser arrays (SALS: 475, 510 and 549\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ under the baseline climate), but the retention is held at 0.905 by the design rule and the ranking is unchanged. SALS exceeds the phenology rule by 0.029, 0.034 and 0.042 in LER under the baseline climate (0.022, 0.032 and 0.051 at +3\,\textdegree C).

\textbf{Which ingredients matter.} Two ablations test the design of SALS at the same training budget. Masking the stress inputs (soil water, ARID, and the maximum temperature, irradiance and ET$_0$ forecasts) leaves the LER unchanged (1.269 vs 1.270 under the baseline climate, 1.297 vs 1.297 at +3\,\textdegree C). Training only on the baseline climate gives 1.268 and 1.291. The improvement over the rules therefore does not depend on stress-aware inputs or on training in warmed climates.

Further sensitivity analyses (floor conditioning, chance-constrained training, forecast errors, layout, rotation limit, harvest-index assumptions and a floor of 66\%) are reported in the Supplementary Material (Section~\ref{S-sec:extra}); none changes the ranking of the methods.

\textbf{Tuned rules.} The phenology rule above was written, not tuned, whereas SALS is trained. We therefore tuned its window on the training-block seasons with the same objective as the other rules (electricity at the density where mean retention equals the floor), first on a coarse $4\times3$ grid and then, as a derivative-free baseline, by CMA-ES over a five-parameter schedule (start, end, two ramp widths and a level; three runs of 200 evaluations, Methods). The grid-tuned window starts at 0.15 instead of 0.05 of the thermal-time requirement (Table~\ref{tab:ctl}). Two of the three CMA-ES runs, one started at the grid-tuned window and one at a random point, reached the same almost binary window (all three runs stopped at the generation cap) from 0.20 to 0.93 of the requirement at level 1.00; the third stopped in a worse optimum. Pooled over three splits and 76 distinct crop--site pairs, the grid-tuned window raises LER over the written rule by 0.026 (95\% cluster-bootstrap interval 0.016 to 0.037), the CMA-ES window by 0.036 (0.023 to 0.052) and SALS by 0.044 (0.030 to 0.060), so the optimisation of a window accounts for about 84\% of the margin of SALS over the written rule. SALS exceeds the CMA-ES window by 0.007 LER (0.002 to 0.014), which is 2.4\% more electricity at equal retention, and the grid-tuned window by 0.018 (0.011 to 0.025). The remaining margin is small (it covers the pairs only: SALS is the mean of three training seeds per split, whose pooled margins over the CMA-ES window are 0.007, 0.008 and 0.007, and the CMA-ES schedule and the grid window were re-tuned on each split's own training block); it is not explained by the tuning of a window or a ramp, and it cannot come from soil water, forecasts or aridity, because masking them leaves SALS unchanged (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}); canopy development and density remain untested. SALS and the CMA-ES window meet the floor in 83\% of seasons, the written, grid-tuned and seasonal rules in 84--85\%. Per split, SALS exceeds the CMA-ES window by 0.008 (95\% interval 0.001 to 0.016; 32 pairs), 0.010 (0.001 to 0.022; 26 pairs) and 0.005 ($-0.002$ to $0.012$; 35 pairs), with per-pair medians of 0.001 to 0.002, so the margin is positive on every split but concentrated in a few pairs. The design step quantises the density to a grid of 0.05 (about 30\% of the mean density), which is much coarser than the margin. We therefore also compared the controllers on the test seasons at matched mean retention, reading the LER at a retention of 0.90 off the retention--LER curve of each pair (interpolated over the grid): SALS exceeds the CMA-ES window by 0.0033 (95\% interval $-0.0004$ to 0.0071; 73 pairs), the grid-tuned window by 0.011 (0.007 to 0.016) and the written rule by 0.036 (0.023 to 0.051). At fixed densities of 0.10 to 0.30 the difference to the CMA-ES window is 0.001 to 0.003 LER, with intervals that include zero. At matched retention the margin over the optimised window is therefore not distinguishable from zero.

\subsection{What the learned policy does}\label{sec:policy}
Averaged over the rainfed test seasons of the main split at a density of 0.3, the light-sharing level of SALS is a smooth function of relative thermal time. It is close to zero in the first 10\% of the thermal time required for maturity, rises through 0.55 at 10--20\%, stays at 0.94--0.99 between 30\% and 70\%, and falls through 0.58 at 80--90\% to about zero at maturity. The plateau overlaps the window in which our crop model penalises shading through the harvest index; we did not test whether this alignment is learned or inherited from the initialisation. The ramps at both ends let the modules produce electricity when additional light is worth least to the crop. Fig.~\ref{fig:trajectory} shows two example seasons. The phenology rule, which is also the initialisation of the network, shares light during the whole growing period. Fine-tuning through the simulator found that sharing can be reduced at the edges of the season: this pattern describes the average policy at a density of 0.3 (the designed mean is 0.17) and we did not test that it causes the gain; the CMA-ES window is almost binary, so a smooth ramp is not needed to reproduce most of it.

The policy also shares less light on days with a forecast heat stress: the mean level is 0.35 on such days against 0.60 (baseline) and 0.51 (+3\,\textdegree C) on other days, and 58--63\% of sites show a lower level on heat days. This cannot be read as a stress response, however. Heat days occur late in the season, when the phenological ramp is already falling, and a policy without any stress input shows a similar contrast (0.37 vs 0.57 in a separate check). The ablation above confirms that stress information does not improve the result.

\subsection{Crops and regions}\label{sec:crops}
The benefit of light sharing differs between crops (baseline climate, SALS). Soybean and maize tolerate the densest arrays (GCR 0.24 and 0.19; 691 and 577\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$; LER 1.41 and 1.30), potato follows (0.19; 542; 1.31), and rice and wheat are the most restrictive (0.13 and 0.15; 395 and 428; 1.20 for both). The stress rule has a lower LER for every crop (1.26 for soybean, 1.14--1.21 for the others).

The floor is not met evenly. Production-weighted retention is 0.888 for soybean and 0.898 for maize, below 0.9, because high-yielding seasons lose more than low-yielding ones. Regionally, Latin America and Oceania (4 sites) combine the highest density (GCR 0.41) and electricity (1090\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$) with a production-weighted retention of only 0.862. Africa and the Middle East (8 sites) reach 0.894, and only 63\% of their sites keep a mean retention of at least 0.9. South Asia, the region with the most sites (39), reaches 0.902 (67\% of sites) at a density of 0.14. The regional figures rest on 4 to 39 pairs without intervals and should be read as indications. A regulatory floor enforced per site would therefore need a region- and crop-specific design step, not a global one.

\subsection{Model behaviour and validation}\label{sec:shade}
\textbf{PV output.} The simulated specific yield of an isolated tracker row agrees with PVGIS 5.2 \citep{pvgis} at 29 sites with $r=0.971$, a mean bias of $-4.9$\% and a mean absolute deviation of 5.5\% (Section~\ref{sec:sim}). At higher ground coverage the shading and diffuse-transposition model was compared with pvlib \citep{holmgren2018} (infinite-sheds model with isotropic sky and backtracking, driven by the same hourly irradiance and sun position at 12 sites spanning the latitudes of the data set, year-round). The simulated plane-of-array irradiance is 0.4\% above pvlib at $g=0.05$ and 0.7, 1.4, 2.1, 2.6 and 3.0\% above it at $g=0.1$, 0.2, 0.3, 0.4 and 0.5 (site ranges within 0.1 to 1.0 points of these means). The loss relative to a sparse array is therefore underestimated: 2.8\% instead of 4.4\% at $g=0.3$ and 6.3\% instead of 8.7\% at $g=0.5$, which favours dense arrays by about 1.5 to 2.5 points of electricity in this range.

\textbf{Shade response.} Fig.~\ref{fig:shade} shows the simulated relative yield of rainfed crops under static arrays as a function of the season-mean shading of the crop. Yield declines close to proportionally with shading: 84--89\% relative yield at 12\% shading, 70--78\% at 25\%, 57--67\% at 37\% and 35--46\% at 57\%. Wheat loses most and potato and maize least. This response is steeper than the \emph{less than proportional} losses that \citet{laub2022} report for small shade levels of C3 cereals and tubers, and it does not reproduce their finding that maize and grain legumes are more shade sensitive than C3 cereals. In the simulator, shading also relieves heat and drought in rainfed maize and soybean, which offsets the higher harvest-index penalty we assigned to them. The absolute effect of shading on each crop is therefore uncertain (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\textbf{Comparison with field trials.} We fitted one radiation-use compensation coefficient per crop to the yield--shading curves of \citet{laub2022} using training-block sites only (Methods, calibration script in the repository). The fit changed wheat and rice only (coefficients 0.4 and 0.1) because the proportional model already lies within about 0.02 of the C3-cereal and tuber curves, and it cannot lower the maize and legume responses below the proportional case (root-mean-square error 0.16 for maize and 0.08 for soybean). Against field trials (not independent of the choice of ensemble, Table~\ref{tab:fieldval}) (Table~\ref{tab:fieldval}) the model underestimates retained yield: rice 0.79 against 0.84 \citep{gonocruz2021}, wheat and potato 0.74 and 0.72 against 0.81--0.82 in the worse season of \citet{weselek2021} (and above 1 in the better one), and maize 0.70--0.75 against 0.83--0.91 at 30--35\% shading \citep{ramosfuentes2023}, where harvest index was not affected by shade. The model is therefore conservative for food retention with respect to these trials, except for the stronger maize losses of the meta-analysis. Table~\ref{S-tab:variants} shows the consequence for the design results: with the calibrated response the allowed density and the LER of every method rise (phenology rule 1.28 against 1.23), and with a response fitted to the field trials the LER of the phenology rule is 1.62. The phenology rule is the best method in all three cases, and light sharing adds LER $+0.17$, $+0.19$ and $+0.30$ over static arrays (conservative, calibrated, field-fitted); only the order of the seasonal and stress rules changes. The absolute gains for rice, wheat and potato are thus conservative with respect to these trials; for maize and soybean the meta-analysis points the other way (Section~\ref{sec:mlresults}).

\subsection{A shade-response model learned from field data}\label{sec:mlresults}
Cross-validation by study shows what the data can and cannot support (Tables~\ref{S-tab:mlcv} and~\ref{S-tab:mlcvtype}). Over all 339 shaded observations the root-mean-square error of a held-out study falls from 0.43 for proportional loss $y=1-s$ to 0.37 for one curve for all crops and to 0.31 for crop-type curves, but this gain comes from crop types that the simulator does not represent (berries, forages, fruits and vegetables: 0.53 to 0.36). For the four crop types that the simulator uses, the learned curves are not better than proportional loss: the error is 0.222 against 0.220 pooled (difference $+0.002$, 95\% interval $-0.023$ to $+0.030$), 0.187 against 0.214 for C3 cereals, and slightly worse for maize, grain legumes and tubers. Partial pooling is as good as separate curves (0.312 against 0.313) and the covariates add almost nothing (0.311). Gradient-boosted trees on the same inputs are worse (0.321) than the parametric model. The error of 0.22 for the four crop types is large: yields of a new study differ from the learned curve by about 20 percentage points, which is the between-study spread that a design under uncertainty has to cover. Crop type is the information that transfers between studies, and none of the covariate effects on $\log\beta$ is clearly different from zero (irradiation $-0.10$, 90\% interval $-0.32$ to $0.09$; subtropical zone $-0.37$, $-0.73$ to $0.07$; intercropping $0.16$, $-0.48$ to $0.45$). The estimated effect of solar-panel shade is not interpretable, because all 46 panel observations are of leafy vegetables, mainly lettuce, and the effect is confounded with crop type.


The learned curves show a clear ordering of crop types (Table~\ref{tab:mlcurves}). At 20\% shading C3 cereals and tubers lose less than proportionally (relative yield 0.93), whereas maize (0.73) and grain legumes (0.71) lose more; at 40\% shading the values are 0.72 and 0.71 against 0.42 and 0.40. Intervals are wide for maize ($0.27$--$0.59$ at 40\%) and for tubers, which rest on two studies. The simulator's responses are close to the data for cereals and potato but, under both calibrated ensembles, too mild for maize at 40\% shading (0.75 for E2 against 0.42 in the data); for soybean the E2 value (0.48) lies within the interval of the data (0.40, 80\% interval 0.34--0.53). The calibration cannot lower the maize response further, because the simulated shading also relieves heat and drought stress and the compensation and harvest-index parameters are bounded below at zero. The field trials of maize are also milder than the meta-analysis: \citet{ramosfuentes2023} report a harvest index unaffected by shade, and \citet{sekiyama2019} a yield $5.6$\% above the control under a low-density stilt-mounted array in one season at one site. For maize and soybean the direction of the bias is uncertain, and the gains reported in this paper are possibly overestimated.

\subsection{Learning under shade-response uncertainty}\label{sec:uncertain}
\textbf{Calibration.} Gradient descent through the simulator fitted the five crops to the data-driven curves and the field trials (ensemble E2, Table~\ref{S-tab:posterior}). The data identify two features: the harvest index of maize is hardly affected by shade (multiplier $0.16\pm0.13$, $c\approx0.14$), as observed by \citet{ramosfuentes2023}, and soybean is more shade sensitive than the cereals (compensation $0.05$, harvest-index multiplier $1.66$), as in \citet{laub2022}. Wheat has the largest compensation ($0.91\pm0.31$) and the largest uncertainty; the compensation is an effective parameter that absorbs diffuse-light and light-saturation effects that the simulator does not model explicitly, not a physical value.


\textbf{Learned controllers.} Controllers were evaluated under four fixed responses on 21 crop--site pairs of split~0 with one training seed, so the intervals below, which are cluster bootstraps over pairs of per-pair mean LER differences, do not include seed or split variation (Table~\ref{tab:robust} gives season means, which differ slightly). Under every response SALS, trained on the proportional response, is better than the written phenology rule: by $+0.019$ ($+0.007$ to $+0.039$), $+0.024$ ($+0.009$ to $+0.046$), $+0.038$ ($+0.018$ to $+0.062$) and $+0.015$ ($+0.001$ to $+0.037$) LER under the proportional, calibrated, field-fitted and harsh responses. It is also above the grid-tuned window and the CMA-ES optimised window, but by less: 0.005 to 0.018 and 0.005 to 0.010 LER, with intervals that exclude zero for the grid-tuned window under 3 of the four responses and for the CMA-ES window under none. Training on the narrow ensemble E1 did not change this: SALS-robust differs from SALS by $-$0.005 to $+$0.003 (intervals contain zero except for one response) and SALS-adaptive, which sees the measured biomass ratio, by $-$0.010 to $+$0.007. Training on the wider ensembles did not help either (the robust controllers fall below SALS even under responses inside their training ensemble, which may point to under-training with our budget of 300 steps): SALS-robust on E2 and E3 and SALS-adaptive on E3 are below SALS by 0.014, 0.017, 0.017 LER under the proportional response, 0.017, 0.021, 0.020 under the calibrated response, 0.004, 0.005, 0.011 under the field-fitted response (intervals contain zero) and 0.008, 0.014, 0.017 under the harsh response; the intervals exclude zero in 7 of the 9 comparisons for the proportional, calibrated and harsh responses. The E3 robust controller is not distinguishable from the written rule ($+$0.001 to $+$0.032) under any response (all intervals contain zero). With one seed and 21 pairs these controllers therefore did not retain the margin of SALS within our training budget; a controller that must perform across plausible responses is less specialised, but a larger budget or more seeds could change this. The feedback rule is worse than the written rule by 0.026--0.058 LER under every response (intervals exclude zero). A plausible reason is that the biomass ratio does not reveal the harvest-index loss from shading around flowering, but we did not test this explanation.

\textbf{Design under uncertainty.} The larger risk is in the design step (Tables~\ref{tab:design} and~\ref{tab:ooe}, Fig.~\ref{fig:designfrontier}). The designs below are evaluated by leaving one ensemble member out as the truth and using the other four, so that the truth never enters the design. Pooled over the three spatial splits (76 distinct crop--site pairs), a density chosen under the mean response of E2 leaves 70\% of the pairs at the floor when the true response is another plausible one, against 87\% for an oracle design (E3: 71\% and 85\%). Choosing the density so that the floor is met in at least 75\% of the other members raises compliance by $+7.9$ points under E2 (95\% cluster-bootstrap interval 4.5 to 11.6) and $+7.9$ under E3 (3.9 to 12.1), at a cost of 0.031 and 0.025 in LER. Raising the floor used in the design from 0.90 to 0.92 under the mean response does more: $+18.0$ points (13.4 to 22.6) at 0.068 LER under E2 and $+14.3$ points (9.5 to 19.2) at 0.056 under E3, and the shortfall is smaller (mean over pairs of the maximum of zero and 0.9 minus retention: 0.0022 against 0.0045 for the quantile rule under E2). The two kinds of rule lie on one frontier (Fig.~\ref{fig:designfrontier}). Interpolating the margin frontier (floors 0.90 to 0.96) at the compliance of the ensemble rules, the LER of the margin minus that of the rule is +0.001 (-0.009 to +0.012) for the rule with 75\% of the other members and +0.003 (-0.016 to +0.021) for the rule with all of them under E2, and -0.006 (-0.019 to +0.005) and +0.024 (-0.011 to +0.058) under E3 (95\% intervals). We find no detectable difference, but the intervals (down to $-0.02$ and up to $+0.06$ LER) are too wide to establish equivalence. The ensemble is thus useful for sizing the margin (0.02 to 0.04 above the floor for 85--97\% of the pairs), not for replacing it. None of these rules is safe outside the ensemble (Table~\ref{tab:ooe}): under the proportional, calibrated and harsh responses the mean-response design leaves 7--17\% of the pairs at the floor, a floor of 0.92 or the ensemble rules 12--33\%, a floor of 0.94 29--47\% and a floor of 0.96 51--78\%, at a cost of about 0.2 LER, whereas the field-fitted response, which lies within the range spanned by the ensembles, is handled well by every rule (80--99\%) except the mean-response design (68--79\%). Outside the ensemble the shortfall of the pairs below the floor is also large: under E2 the mean shortfall per pair (maximum of zero and 0.9 minus retention) is 0.075, 0.060 and 0.104 for the mean-response design under the proportional, calibrated and harsh responses, against 0.053, 0.038 and 0.078 for a floor of 0.92 and 0.012, 0.007 and 0.024 for a floor of 0.96. Across the five members of an ensemble, the standard deviation of the simulated relative yield of a crop (static arrays, baseline climate, held-out sites of split~0) is 0.02 to 0.04 at 20\% shading and 0.02 to 0.06 at 40\% under E2, and 0.02 to 0.09 and 0.03 to 0.15 under E3 (largest for soybean and potato; ranges between the extreme members up to 0.36), against a leave-one-study-out error of 0.22 for new studies. The two quantities differ (a spread of curves against an error over observations), but the ensembles are narrower than the between-study spread for all crops except, possibly, soybean under E3. The pooled shares hide large differences between crops (Table~\ref{S-tab:ooecrop}, E2, 10 to 20 pairs per crop). Under the proportional, calibrated and harsh responses the design under the mean response and the rule that requires 75\% of the members leave no wheat, rice or potato pair at the floor and at most 12\% of the maize pairs (a floor of 0.92 raises this to at most 10\% of the rice pairs and 18\% of the maize pairs), whereas 90 to 100\% of the soybean pairs meet it, because the ensemble already contains steep soybean responses (the oracle design reaches 90\% for soybean and 65 to 100\% for the other crops). Even a floor of 0.96 leaves 0 to 12\% of the wheat pairs at the floor under the harsh and proportional responses and 42\% of the potato pairs. The out-of-ensemble failure is thus a failure for cereals, tubers and maize, and the pooled values of 7 to 33\% mix crops that fail completely with a crop that does not fail. The guarantee therefore holds only within the range of responses that the ensemble spans; we did not test an ensemble that spans the proportional and harsh responses, which would protect against them at a higher cost in LER.

\subsection{Economics of light sharing}\label{sec:cost}
All figures in this section follow from the assumed parameters of Table~\ref{tab:costparams} and should be read as a sensitivity analysis, not a forecast. The economics are dominated by the capacity installed, not by the crop (Table~\ref{tab:cost}): the annuity and operating cost of the modules is 11--32 kUSD per hectare and year, whereas the crop revenue lost is about 0.3 kUSD, so a method is worth what it earns per installed watt. With the densities designed for the floor, static arrays earn $+0.3$ kUSD\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ at 60\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ and break even at 59, whereas all light-sharing methods lose money at that price ($-3$ to $-10$ kUSD) and break even at 70 (stress rule), 73 (SALS), 73 (CMA-ES window), 77 (grid-tuned window), 80 (written rule) and 92 (seasonal sharing) USD\,MWh$^{-1}$. The 95\% cluster-bootstrap intervals over pairs are 56--61 for static arrays, 69--76 for SALS, 70--77 for the CMA-ES window and 75--84 for the written rule, and 46--50 for the plant (break-even prices of SALS and the CMA-ES window differ by $-0.4$, $-1.1$ to 0.4); they cover the sampling of pairs and not the cost assumptions. At 90 USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ static arrays (6.3 kUSD) are better than the written (4.0) and grid-tuned (5.5) rules, close to the CMA-ES window (7.3) and worse than SALS (7.6), which overtakes static arrays above about 85 USD\,MWh$^{-1}$; the difference between SALS and static arrays at 90 USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ is $+1.3$ kUSD (95\% interval $-0.04$ to $2.7$, 76 pairs) and not distinguishable from zero, whereas SALS exceeds the CMA-ES window by 0.3 ($-0.1$ to 0.8, not distinguishable from zero), the grid-tuned window by 2.1 (1.6 to 2.7) and the written rule by 3.6 (2.8 to 4.5). A conventional plant that replaces the crop breaks even at 48 USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ and is more profitable than every agrivoltaic option above that price (16 kUSD at 60, 94 kUSD at 120, against $-6$ and 21 for SALS); below it all options lose money and static arrays lose least (at 30 USD\,MWh$^{-1}$: plant $-23.2$, SALS $-18.7$, static $-5.7$ kUSD). Per megawatt-hour, the electricity of SALS costs about 73 USD including the crop lost, against 48 USD for the plant, a difference of 25 USD\,MWh$^{-1}$. A premium of that size would equalise the unit cost but not the profit per hectare, because a hectare of plant produces three times as much electricity (1306 against 438 MWh): for the same electricity as one hectare of plant, SALS needs about three hectares of array, which costs about 25 USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ more including the crop lost but keeps about 90\% of the yield of that cropland; relative to the plant, SALS would need a premium of about 49 USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ at a market price of 60 and about 109 at 90 to match its profit per hectare, and only about 13 at 60 (none above 73) to match the open field. The sensitivity is large (Table~\ref{S-tab:costsens}), and the ranking of static arrays and light sharing flips with the module cost (at 1.5 USD\,Wp$^{-1}$ static arrays are better than SALS, at 0.7 SALS leads by 7 kUSD): at 90 USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ the net value of SALS changes from $+17$ to $-8$ kUSD between module costs of 0.7 and 1.5 USD\,Wp$^{-1}$, whereas crop prices matter little.

Because the density was designed for the floor and not for profit, we also let each site choose, among the densities that meet the floor in its history, the one that maximises its historical net value (sites where none pays stay open field; Fig.~\ref{fig:costopt}). Then no method pays at 30--40 USD\,MWh$^{-1}$; at 60 14--59\% of the pairs install an array (none for seasonal sharing) and the net values are small (static arrays 0.9, SALS 0.3 kUSD); static arrays are the best option up to about 75 USD\,MWh$^{-1}$, SALS overtakes them at about 75 (4.9 against 4.3 at 80, 8.3 against 6.3 at 90, 20.7 against 12.3 at 120), the CMA-ES window at about 78, the stress rule at about 81, the grid-tuned window at about 87 and the written rule at about 97. The value of control is therefore real but depends on a high price: at 90 USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ and with profit-optimal densities SALS exceeds the CMA-ES window by 0.4 kUSD\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ (95\% cluster-bootstrap interval 0.01 to 0.8), the grid-tuned window by 1.7 (1.2 to 2.2), the written rule by 2.8 (2.0 to 3.6) and static arrays by 2.0 (0.8 to 3.3); the CMA-ES window exceeds static arrays by about 1.6 (0.4 to 2.8), so most of the value of control is obtained with a well-optimised rule.

\textbf{Cost of the safety margin.} At 90\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$, moving from a density designed under the mean response to a floor of 0.92 under that response costs 1.00 kUSD\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ (17\%) under E2 for 18 more points of compliance (70\% to 88\%) and 1.13 kUSD (20\%) under E3 for 14 points (71\% to 85\%), about 56 and 79 USD\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ per point. The ensemble rule that requires all other members to meet the floor costs 0.85 and 1.22 kUSD for 19 and 17 points (45 and 74 USD per point), so on this measure it is cheaper than the margin under E2 and similar under E3. At 60\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ a margin has a negative cost, because every module beyond break-even loses money; the economics of the design margin therefore depend on the price level, whereas its effect on compliance does not.

\subsection{Agrivoltaics under warming}
Under warming, the open-field production of the held-out sites (Fig.~\ref{fig:warming}) falls at +3\,\textdegree C by 23\% for maize and 18\% for rice, is nearly unchanged for soybean, and rises for wheat (+3\%) and potato (+10\%). Agrivoltaic production with light-sharing control follows the open field at about 90\% of its level, so it inherits the same climate impacts (maize $-31$\%, rice $-27$\% relative to the baseline open field at +3\,\textdegree C). Agrivoltaics does not protect staple production against warming in this model. The LER of light-sharing systems increases slightly with warming (SALS 1.270 to 1.297; phenology rule 1.236 to 1.265), because the floor allows slightly denser arrays in warmer conditions (GCR 0.173 to 0.177 and electricity 510 to 540\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$). The margin of SALS over the rules does not grow with warming (Table~\ref{S-tab:stats}).

\subsection{Global potential and what it would take}\label{sec:global}
Every site is evaluated when it is held out (222 of the 400 crop--site pairs are held out in at least one split, and three of them have no valid season, leaving 219), and each crop's mean electricity per hectare is scaled to its physical area in SPAM 2010 (581\,Mha for the five crops, which avoids counting multiple-cropped land twice). Equipping 5\% of this area (29.1\,Mha) with SALS arrays at the floor would produce 14.4\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ under the baseline climate and 16.0\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ at +3\,\textdegree C, with a production-weighted retention of 0.903 and 0.900. The phenology rule would give 13.0 and 14.5\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$, and static arrays 6.4 and 6.9\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$. For comparison, world electricity generation was about 30\,PWh in 2023 \citep{ember2024}.

These numbers require large amounts of hardware. SALS at 5\% coverage needs 10.6\,TWp of modules (11.3\,TWp at +3\,\textdegree C), at a capacity factor of 15.5\% (16.2\%). World solar PV capacity at the end of 2024 was about 1.9\,TW \citep{irena2025}, so the requirement is about 5.6 times the installed PV fleet. The electricity is also variable, peaks at midday and would be curtailed or stored. The figures are technical potentials, not projections. Ordering sites by electricity per hectare gives a supply curve (Fig.~\ref{fig:supply}): the best 2\% of the staple cropland would supply about 21\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ and the best 4.7\% about 40\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ with SALS. The best tenth of the sites are mostly rainfed maize and soybean sites in the Americas and in southern and eastern Africa (7\% mean irrigated fraction against 29\% overall), which are also among the regions where the floor is hardest to meet (Section~\ref{sec:crops}). Siting by electricity alone would therefore concentrate agrivoltaics where food-security risk is highest.

Maize and soybean, for which the simulated shade response is milder than the meta-analysis (Section~\ref{sec:mlresults}), account for 53\% of the electricity of the upscaling (a scaling convention of 5\% of the cropland, reported without an interval) (maize 30\%, soybean 23\%, wheat 29\%, rice 15\%, potato 3\%). Counting only wheat, rice and potato would give about 7\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ at the same coverage, so the global figure should be read as an upper estimate for about half of its content. By crop, the LER of SALS under the baseline climate is 1.20 for wheat and rice, 1.31 for potato, 1.30 for maize and 1.41 for soybean.
