\subsection{Electricity and food on held-out sites and years}\label{sec:main}
Table~\ref{tab:main} compares the four controllers on the 92 held-out sites of the main split (seasons sown in 2015--2019), after each site's density has been chosen from its 2001--2014 seasons with the same rule for all controllers. By construction all methods keep the mean yield retention close to 90\% (0.904--0.917), so differences in electricity are differences in the density that each controller can sustain under this floor.

\textbf{Static arrays are the weakest.} Without light sharing, the floor limits the ground coverage to 6\% on average, and arrays produce 217\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$, 16\% of a conventional PV plant per hectare (LER 1.07). Rotating the modules to let light through during the cropping season (seasonal sharing) allows a coverage of 17\% and 397\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ (LER 1.19). A rule that shares light only while the crop is growing (phenology rule) reaches 465\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ (LER 1.24). An expert rule that additionally shades the crop on forecast heat or drought days (stress rule) is worse than the phenology rule (LER 1.18): its density is limited to 13\% because it reduces light at times when the floor is most binding.

\textbf{The learned controller is best by a modest, consistent margin.} SALS sustains a density of 17\% (similar to the phenology rule), produces 510\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ and reaches an LER of 1.270 at the same retention. Compared with the phenology rule this is +0.038 in LER (95\% CI over sites 0.025--0.053; +10\% electricity) and it is higher at 84\% of the 92 sites (Table~\ref{tab:stats}). Compared with static arrays it is +0.205 (0.176--0.236), with 2.35 times the electricity at 2.9 times the density. The frontier in Fig.~\ref{fig:frontier} shows the same picture at matched densities: the learned policy lies on or slightly above the phenology rule over the whole density range, both lie well above seasonal sharing and the stress rule, and static arrays are far below.

\textbf{What the floor does and does not guarantee.} Enforcing the floor on the historical mean does not guarantee it in every season. In the test seasons, 70\% of SALS seasons have $r\ge0.9$ (phenology rule 69\%, stress rule 76\%, static arrays 93\%), and 2.7\% have $r<0.8$ (1.3\%, 0.9\% and 0.9\%). The lower tail of SALS is therefore slightly heavier than that of the rules: its 10th percentile of $r$ is 0.883, against 0.887, 0.886 and 0.903. Requiring $r\ge0.9$ in at least 80\% of a site's historical seasons (chance-constrained design) raises the season-level compliance to 90--94\% for the light-sharing controllers. It costs about 0.065 in LER for SALS (1.205), which remains the best controller (phenology rule 1.192; Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}).

\subsection{Robustness}\label{sec:sensitivity}
\textbf{Seeds and splits.} Three training seeds of SALS give LERs of 1.270, 1.268 and 1.269 under the baseline climate and 1.297, 1.298 and 1.297 at +3\,\textdegree C. On two further spatial splits, in which different blocks of sites are held out, SALS exceeds the phenology rule by 0.047 and 0.041 in LER under the baseline climate and by 0.052 and 0.039 at +3\,\textdegree C (main split: 0.034 and 0.032). In every split SALS ranks first, the phenology rule second and static arrays last.

\textbf{Canopy cooling.} The canopy-cooling coefficient $\kappa$ is the most uncertain microclimate parameter. We repeated the whole protocol, including the historical design step, with $\kappa=0$, 1 and 2\,\textdegree C. Electricity changes with $\kappa$ because cooler canopies tolerate denser arrays (SALS: 475, 510 and 549\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$ under the baseline climate), but the retention is held at 0.905 by the design rule and the ranking is unchanged. SALS exceeds the phenology rule by 0.029, 0.034 and 0.042 in LER under the baseline climate (0.022, 0.032 and 0.051 at +3\,\textdegree C).

\textbf{Which ingredients matter.} Two ablations test the design of SALS at the same training budget. Masking the stress inputs (soil water, ARID, and the maximum temperature, irradiance and ET$_0$ forecasts) leaves the LER unchanged (1.269 vs 1.270 under the baseline climate, 1.297 vs 1.297 at +3\,\textdegree C). Training only on the baseline climate gives 1.268 and 1.291. The improvement over the rules therefore does not depend on stress-aware inputs or on training in warmed climates.

\subsection{What the learned policy does}\label{sec:policy}
Averaged over the rainfed test seasons of the main split at a density of 0.3, the light-sharing level of SALS is a smooth function of relative thermal time. It is close to zero in the first 10\% of the thermal time required for maturity, rises through 0.55 at 10--20\%, stays at 0.94--0.99 between 30\% and 70\%, and falls through 0.58 at 80--90\% to about zero at maturity. The plateau covers the window in which our crop model penalises shading through the harvest index. The ramps at both ends let the modules produce electricity when additional light is worth least to the crop. Fig.~\ref{fig:trajectory} shows two example seasons. The phenology rule, which is also the initialisation of the network, shares light during the whole growing period. Fine-tuning through the simulator found that sharing can be reduced at the edges of the season: SALS gains its advantage by yielding light when it matters and collecting electricity when it matters less.

The policy also shares less light on days with a forecast heat stress: the mean level is 0.35 on such days against 0.60 (baseline) and 0.51 (+3\,\textdegree C) on other days, and 58--63\% of sites show a lower level on heat days. This cannot be read as a stress response, however. Heat days occur late in the season, when the phenological ramp is already falling, and a policy without any stress input shows a similar contrast (0.37 vs 0.57 in a separate check). The ablation above confirms that stress information does not improve the result.

\subsection{Crops and regions}\label{sec:crops}
The benefit of light sharing differs between crops (baseline climate, SALS). Soybean and maize tolerate the densest arrays (GCR 0.24 and 0.19; 691 and 577\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$; LER 1.41 and 1.30), potato follows (0.19; 542; 1.31), and rice and wheat are the most restrictive (0.13 and 0.15; 395 and 428; 1.20 for both). The stress rule has a lower LER for every crop (1.26 for soybean, 1.14--1.21 for the others).

The floor is not met evenly. Production-weighted retention is 0.888 for soybean and 0.898 for maize, below 0.9, because high-yielding seasons lose more than low-yielding ones. Regionally, Latin America and Oceania (4 sites) combine the highest density (GCR 0.41) and electricity (1090\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$) with a production-weighted retention of only 0.862. Africa and the Middle East (8 sites) reach 0.894, and only 63\% of their sites keep a mean retention of at least 0.9. South Asia, the region with the most sites (39), reaches 0.902 (67\% of sites) at a density of 0.14. A regulatory floor enforced per site would therefore need a region- and crop-specific design step, not a global one.

\subsection{Model behaviour and validation}\label{sec:shade}
\textbf{PV output.} The simulated specific yield of an isolated tracker row agrees with PVGIS 5.2 \citep{pvgis} at 29 sites with $r=0.971$, a mean bias of $-4.9$\% and a mean absolute deviation of 5.5\% (Section~\ref{sec:sim}).

\textbf{Shade response.} Fig.~\ref{fig:shade} shows the simulated relative yield of rainfed crops under static arrays as a function of the season-mean shading of the crop. Yield declines close to proportionally with shading: 84--89\% relative yield at 12\% shading, 70--78\% at 25\%, 57--67\% at 37\% and 35--46\% at 57\%. Wheat loses most and potato and maize least. This response is steeper than the \emph{less than proportional} losses that \citet{laub2022} report for small shade levels of C3 cereals and tubers, and it does not reproduce their finding that maize and grain legumes are more shade sensitive than C3 cereals. In the simulator, shading also relieves heat and drought in rainfed maize and soybean, which offsets the higher harvest-index penalty we assigned to them. The absolute effect of shading on each crop is therefore uncertain (Section~\ref{sec:limits}).

\textbf{Comparison with field trials.} We fitted one radiation-use compensation coefficient per crop to the yield--shading curves of \citet{laub2022} using training-block sites only (Methods, calibration script in the repository). The fit changed wheat and rice only (coefficients 0.4 and 0.1) because the proportional model already lies within about 0.02 of the C3-cereal and tuber curves, and it cannot lower the maize and legume responses below the proportional case (root-mean-square error 0.16 for maize and 0.08 for soybean). Against independent field trials (Table~\ref{tab:fieldval}) the model underestimates retained yield: rice 0.79 against 0.84 \citep{gonocruz2021}, wheat and potato 0.74 and 0.72 against 0.81--0.82 in the worse season of \citet{weselek2021} (and above 1 in the better one), and maize 0.70--0.75 against 0.83--0.91 at 30--35\% shading \citep{ramosfuentes2023}, where harvest index was not affected by shade. The model is therefore conservative for food retention with respect to these trials, except for the stronger maize losses of the meta-analysis. Table~\ref{tab:variants} shows the consequence for the design results: with the calibrated response the allowed density and the LER of every method rise (phenology rule 1.28 against 1.23), and with a response fitted to the field trials the LER of the phenology rule is 1.62. The phenology rule is the best method in all three cases, and light sharing adds LER $+0.17$, $+0.19$ and $+0.30$ over static arrays (conservative, calibrated, field-fitted); only the order of the seasonal and stress rules changes. The absolute gains reported in this paper are thus lower bounds with respect to these trials and not upper bounds.

\subsection{A shade-response model learned from field data}\label{sec:mlresults}
The curve learned for each crop type predicts new studies better than a proportional loss (Table~\ref{tab:mlcv}). In leave-one-study-out cross-validation the root-mean-square error falls from 0.43 for $y=1-s$ to 0.37 for one curve for all crops and to 0.31 for crop-type curves, a reduction of 28\% relative to the proportional loss. Partial pooling is as good as separate curves (0.312 against 0.313), and the covariates add almost nothing (0.311). Gradient-boosted trees on the same inputs are worse (0.321) than the parametric hierarchical model, as expected for 58 studies. Crop type is therefore the information that transfers between studies. None of the covariate effects on $\log\beta$ is clearly different from zero (irradiation $-0.10$, 90\% interval $-0.32$ to $0.09$; subtropical zone $-0.37$, $-0.73$ to $0.07$; intercropping $0.16$, $-0.48$ to $0.45$). The estimated effect of solar-panel shade is not interpretable, because all 46 panel observations are of leafy vegetables, mainly lettuce, and the effect is confounded with crop type.

\input{tables/mlcv}

The learned curves show a clear ordering of crop types (Table~\ref{tab:mlcurves}). At 20\% shading C3 cereals and tubers lose less than proportionally (relative yield 0.93), whereas maize (0.73) and grain legumes (0.71) lose more; at 40\% shading the values are 0.72 and 0.71 against 0.42 and 0.40. Intervals are wide for maize ($0.27$--$0.59$ at 40\%) and for tubers, which rest on two studies. The simulator's responses are close to the data for cereals and potato but, under both calibrated ensembles, too mild for maize and soybean at 40\% shading (0.75 and 0.48 for E2 against 0.42 and 0.40 in the data). The calibration cannot lower the maize response further, because the simulated shading also relieves heat and drought stress and the compensation and harvest-index parameters are bounded below at zero. The field trials of maize \citep{ramosfuentes2023} are also milder than the meta-analysis. For maize and soybean the gains reported in this paper are therefore probably overestimated.

\subsection{Learning under shade-response uncertainty}\label{sec:uncertain}
\textbf{Calibration.} Gradient descent through the simulator fitted the five crops to the data-driven curves and the field trials (ensemble E2, Table~\ref{tab:posterior}). The data identify two features: the harvest index of maize is hardly affected by shade (multiplier $0.16\pm0.13$, $c\approx0.14$), as observed by \citet{ramosfuentes2023}, and soybean is more shade sensitive than the cereals (compensation $0.05$, harvest-index multiplier $1.66$), as in \citet{laub2022}. Wheat has the largest compensation ($0.91\pm0.31$) and the largest uncertainty.

\input{tables/posterior}

\textbf{Learned controllers.} Controllers were trained on E1 and evaluated under four fixed responses. Under every response the learned controller stays better than the best hand-written rule, but only slightly (Table~\ref{tab:robust}): the LER gain of SALS over the phenology rule is $+0.014$, $+0.015$, $+0.033$ and $+0.010$ under the proportional, calibrated, field-fitted and harsh responses. Training on the ensemble did not help: SALS-robust differs from SALS, which was trained on the proportional response only, by at most 0.005 in LER, and SALS-adaptive, which sees the measured biomass ratio, by at most 0.008. The feedback rule is worse than the phenology rule by 0.025--0.064 in LER. A plausible reason is that the biomass ratio does not reveal the harvest-index loss from shading around flowering, so the information that the controller could use to adapt is not in the measurement.

\input{tables/robust}

\textbf{Design under uncertainty.} The larger risk is in the design step (Table~\ref{tab:design}). When the density is chosen under the mean response of E2 and the true response is a different plausible one, the share of sites whose mean retention reaches the floor is 63\% (phenology rule) and 62\% (SALS), against 79\% and 73\% for an oracle design. Choosing the density so that the floor is met in at least 75\% of the plausible responses raises site compliance to 73\% and 71\%, which recovers most of the gap to the oracle, at a cost of 0.042 and 0.040 in LER relative to the design under the mean response. With the earlier ensemble E1 the same design restored the oracle level (76\% and 74\%, against 58\% and 55\% under the mean) at a cost of 0.029 and 0.023 LER. For the proportional and harsh responses, which lie outside the ensembles, the design does not restore compliance (about 20--24\% of sites under E2), so the guarantee holds only within the range of responses that the data support.

\subsection{Agrivoltaics under warming}
Under warming, the open-field production of the held-out sites (Fig.~\ref{fig:warming}) falls at +3\,\textdegree C by 23\% for maize and 18\% for rice, is nearly unchanged for soybean, and rises for wheat (+3\%) and potato (+10\%). Agrivoltaic production with light-sharing control follows the open field at about 90\% of its level, so it inherits the same climate impacts (maize $-31$\%, rice $-27$\% relative to the baseline open field at +3\,\textdegree C). Agrivoltaics does not protect staple production against warming in this model. The LER of light-sharing systems increases slightly with warming (SALS 1.270 to 1.297; phenology rule 1.236 to 1.265), because the floor allows slightly denser arrays in warmer conditions (GCR 0.173 to 0.177 and electricity 510 to 540\,MWh\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$). The margin of SALS over the rules does not grow with warming (Table~\ref{tab:stats}).

\subsection{Global potential and what it would take}\label{sec:global}
Every site is evaluated when it is held out (219 of the 400 crop--site pairs, in at least one split), and each crop's mean electricity per hectare is scaled to its physical area in SPAM 2010 (581\,Mha for the five crops, which avoids counting multiple-cropped land twice). Equipping 5\% of this area (29.1\,Mha) with SALS arrays at the floor would produce 14.4\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ under the baseline climate and 16.0\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ at +3\,\textdegree C, with a production-weighted retention of 0.903 and 0.900. The phenology rule would give 13.0 and 14.5\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$, and static arrays 6.4 and 6.9\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$. For comparison, world electricity generation was about 30\,PWh in 2023 \citep{ember2024}.

These numbers require large amounts of hardware. SALS at 5\% coverage needs 10.6\,TWp of modules (11.3\,TWp at +3\,\textdegree C), at a capacity factor of 15.5\% (16.2\%). World solar PV capacity at the end of 2024 was about 1.9\,TW \citep{irena2025}, so the requirement is about 5.7 times the installed PV fleet. The electricity is also variable, peaks at midday and would be curtailed or stored. The figures are technical potentials, not projections. Ordering sites by electricity per hectare gives a supply curve (Fig.~\ref{fig:supply}): the best 2\% of the staple cropland would supply 16\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ and the best 5\% 40\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ with SALS. The best tenth of the sites are mostly rainfed maize and soybean sites in the Americas and in southern and eastern Africa (7\% mean irrigated fraction against 29\% overall), which are also among the regions where the floor is hardest to meet (Section~\ref{sec:crops}). Siting by electricity alone would therefore concentrate agrivoltaics where food-security risk is highest.
