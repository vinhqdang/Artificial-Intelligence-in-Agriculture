\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the results establish}
Sharing light with the crop is the main lever on the electricity that tracker-based agrivoltaic arrays can produce under a yield floor: with static trackers the floor limits arrays to about 6\% ground coverage (LER 1.07), and any light-sharing control allows 2.1 to 2.9 times the density (LER 1.18--1.24 for written rules). The best simple rule follows crop phenology, needs only the crop's thermal time, which is observable on the farm, and is insensitive to forecast errors, whereas a rule that reacts to heat and drought stress is worse in every climate. The early hypothesis that stress-aware control would be a main source of benefit under warming was not supported: a learned controller without stress inputs performs as well as one with them, and agrivoltaics does not protect staple production against warming in the model, because the systems produce about 90\% of the open-field yield in every climate and follow the same decline in maize and rice. These results hold for the proportional-growth shade response of the main analysis and for the alternative responses we tested (Supplement), but the gains for maize and soybean, which supply about half of the upscaled electricity, are the least certain.

\subsection{What learning adds}\label{sec:ai}
We tested four ways in which learning could matter, and each gives a small or null return. \emph{Learning the shade response from data} is useful mainly as a measurement of how little can be learned: a hierarchical model fitted to 428 observations predicts a held-out study of the four staple crop types to 0.22 in relative yield, no better than proportional loss, and its gain over proportional loss comes from crop types that the simulator does not represent. The response of the staple crops is a distribution with a spread of about 20 percentage points between studies and not a curve. \emph{Calibration through the differentiable simulator} produced an ensemble of plausible responses that fits the trials and the curves for cereals and potato but not those of maize, and it quantifies the design risk. \emph{Control} gains little: a network of 5441 weights trained through whole seasons exceeds the written rule by 0.044 LER, but a window optimised by a derivative-free search (five parameters, 200 simulator runs) is only 0.007 LER behind it, and at matched retention the difference is not distinguishable from zero; we did not test whether such a search would work on the network itself. A reader who needs a simple, auditable controller can use the window and obtain about 83\% of the gain. \emph{Learning the design margin} gave a guarantee but no better frontier: conformal calibration attains a stated compliance within the ensemble, with margins that can be set per crop, and a neural design policy does no better than a simple margin, and neither is safe outside the ensemble. The honest conclusion for the use of artificial intelligence here is that differentiable simulation makes calibration, control and design tractable, and that the data, not the learner, limit the result: the uncertainty of the crop response that enters the design costs more compliance than any controller gains.

\subsection{Implications for agricultural systems and decisions}
Holding the mean retention at 90\% does not hold it in each season or at each site. About 30\% of seasons with light-sharing control fall short of the floor on the main split, the shortfall is mostly small (2.7\% of SALS seasons are below 0.8 of the open-field yield), and production-weighted retention is below 0.9 for soybean and maize and in several regions. A floor enforced per site would therefore need a crop- and site-specific design step, and a margin that is calibrated rather than chosen by hand. Compliance is sensitive to the response assumed in the design: a density chosen under the mean response leaves 71\% of pairs at the floor when another plausible response holds, a margin or a conformal calibration repairs this inside the ensemble, and none of them protects against responses outside it, notably for cereals and tubers when the ensemble is anchored on mild responses. Because all controllers are designed to the same mean retention, their compliance is close by construction, so the choice of controller and the choice of the design belief are different decisions. Economically, light sharing pays only at high electricity prices (break-even 70--92\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ against 59 for static arrays and 48 for a conventional plant under the assumed costs), so a policy that wants dual use of farmland has to price the electricity, the land-use rule or the food that the model does not value. The risk to the farmer is mainly one of compliance with the floor and of the contract, since the crop revenue at stake (about 0.3\,kUSD\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$) is small against the electricity revenue, and it concentrates where the energy gain is largest, in rainfed maize and soybean areas, where the shade response is least certain.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
All results come from a simulator. The PV model agrees with PVGIS at low density and with pvlib to within 3\% of plane-of-array irradiance up to a ground coverage of 0.5, but it underestimates the shading loss of dense arrays by 1.5 to 2.5 points, which changes the LER by at most 0.008 and no ranking, and no measured array output at these densities was used. The shade response was learned from one meta-analysis and compared with four field trials; for maize the data and the simulator disagree (0.42 against 0.75 at 40\% shading), the harvest-index sensitivities are assumptions, and the sensitivity analyses use subsamples of pairs. The floor of 0.9 is a modelling convention and not a regulatory threshold (DIN SPEC 91434 uses 66\%). Canopy cooling is one coefficient applied to a heat-stress function calibrated on air temperature; SIMPLE has a fixed harvest index and no emergence, early canopy closure, weeds or machinery effects; the climate scenarios are deltas that isolate the thermal signal; and the layouts are single-axis trackers over flat fields, with no east--west, vertical or fixed-tilt arrays. The economic analysis uses assumed parameters and a plant cheaper per watt than the agrivoltaic structure, and omits land rent, degradation, extra structure cost, curtailment and subsidies. The conformal and learned design results come from five ensemble members and 76 pairs, with split-level deviations of up to three points from the stated compliance, and the controller comparisons use one network per training seed. The regional and global figures are indications and a scaling convention without intervals. Future work should validate the shade response under trackers for maize and legumes, extend the crop model to grain-number formation and canopy energy balance, add costs and grid constraints, and pursue controllers and designs that guarantee the floor per site and per season.
