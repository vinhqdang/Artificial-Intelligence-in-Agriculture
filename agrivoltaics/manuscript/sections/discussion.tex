\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the results establish}
Four findings stand on consistent evidence. First, sharing light with the crop is the main lever on the electricity that tracker-based agrivoltaic arrays can produce under a yield floor. With static trackers the floor limits arrays to about 6\% ground coverage and an LER of 1.07. Any light-sharing control allows about three times the density, and the LER rises to 1.18--1.24 for hand-written rules. Second, the best simple rule follows crop phenology. It shares light across the middle of the season, around flowering and grain filling, and produces electricity at the beginning and the end. It needs only the crop's thermal time, which is observable on the farm, and it is insensitive to forecast errors, whereas the rule that reacts to heat and drought stress is worse in every climate and degrades with forecast error. Third, the qualitative result does not depend on the weakest part of the model. The simulated shade response is conservative against the field trials we could use (Section~\ref{sec:shade}); calibrating it raises the allowed density, and the phenology rule stays the best method, with a gain over static arrays of 0.17, 0.19 and 0.30 LER under the conservative, calibrated and field-fitted responses. However, the response learned from the meta-analysis shows steeper losses for maize and grain legumes than the simulator reproduces (Section~\ref{sec:mlresults}), so the absolute gains are lower bounds for cereals and potato and probably overestimates for maize and soybean. Fourth, a controller learned through the differentiable simulator is better than the best rule we could write, by 0.04 in LER against the written rule (0.018 against a rule whose window was tuned on the training seasons, 5.7\% more electricity at equal retention), reproducibly over training seeds (range 0.002 in LER), three spatial splits, three canopy-cooling assumptions and four climates, and at 84--86\% of the held-out sites. This gain is small compared with the gap between static and dynamic control.

\subsection{What role does artificial intelligence play?}\label{sec:ai}
We tested three ways in which learning could matter, and the answers differ. The first is learning the crop's response to shade from data. The learned model is useful mainly as a measurement of how little can be learned: a hierarchical model fitted to 428 observations from 58 studies predicts a held-out study of the four staple crop types with an error of 0.22 in relative yield, no better than proportional loss, and the gain over proportional loss (0.43 to 0.31 over all types) comes from crop types that the simulator does not represent. Crop type is the information that transfers, whereas irradiation, climate zone and shade type add nothing measurable and a non-parametric model (gradient-boosted trees) is worse with this number of studies (Section~\ref{sec:mlresults}). Two things follow. The shade response of the staple crops is a distribution with a spread of about 20 percentage points between studies and not a curve, and the process-based simulator does not reproduce the steep losses of maize and legumes seen in the data. Learning the response was limited by the data and not by the model: two studies support the tuber curve, no rice study exists, and 46 panel observations are all leafy vegetables.

The second is calibration through the differentiable simulator. Gradient descent through whole seasons fitted the response of each crop to the learned curves and to the field trials, and it produced an ensemble of plausible responses that reconciles them (Section~\ref{sec:uncertain}). The ensemble informs the design decision: a density chosen under the mean response leaves about 70\% of crop--site pairs at the floor if another plausible response holds, against 85--87\% for an oracle, and a density chosen to meet the floor in 75\% of plausible responses recovers about half of that gap at a cost of 0.04 LER. A simple margin, however, does better: raising the floor from 0.90 to 0.92 under the mean response reaches 85--89\% of the pairs and keeps 0.02--0.04 more LER than the ensemble rule at equal compliance. What the ensemble adds is the size of the margin, not a better rule. The bottleneck of tracker-based agrivoltaics under a food-security floor is the uncertainty of the crop response that enters the design, and it is data on that response, not larger networks, that would improve the result; the learned curves show a spread of about 20 percentage points between studies. The guarantee holds only within the range of responses that the ensemble spans.

The third is control, and here learning contributes little. The learned controller adds 0.018 LER over a tuned phenology rule (and 0.044 over the written one), and 0.01--0.03 over the written rule under the alternative responses on one split; a reader who needs a simple, auditable controller can use that rule and obtain most of the electricity gain over static arrays. Neither of the two extensions that we expected to matter under uncertainty did: training on an ensemble of responses did not change the policy, and neither did giving the controller a farm-measurable signal, the biomass of the crop relative to a reference plot, nor a feedback rule built on that signal. The learned policy also has to be initialised from the phenology rule (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). We therefore do not claim that learned control is what makes agrivoltaics work: in this model, the schedule that a learned policy converges to is a smooth function of crop phenology, which a rule can already express.

\subsection{What the results do not establish}
The early hypothesis that stress-aware control, that is, shading the crop on heat and drought days, would be a main source of benefit under warming was not supported. The stress rule performs worse than the phenology rule in every climate, and a learned controller without stress inputs performs as well as one with them. A plausible reason is that in our crop model the yield gain from shading on a hot or dry day is smaller than the loss of light on that day, so stress information does not help. Whether this holds for crop models with more detailed canopy-temperature and heat-stress responses is an open question. Agrivoltaics does not protect staple production against warming in our simulations: light-sharing systems produce about 90\% of the open-field yield in every climate and follow the same decline in maize and rice (Fig.~\ref{fig:warming}).

\subsection{Food security in practice}
Holding the historical mean retention at 90\% does not hold it in each season or at each site. 70\% of SALS seasons meet the floor, the lower tail is slightly heavier than for the rules, and production-weighted retention is below 0.9 for soybean (0.888) and maize (0.898) and in several regions. Chance-constrained design raises season-level compliance to about 92\% at a cost of 0.065 in LER, and the ranking of the controllers is preserved. Whichever formulation is chosen, a per-site regulatory floor would need a region- and crop-specific design step. The upscaled potential (Section~\ref{sec:global}) shows the trade-off in magnitude. The 14\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ that 5\% of the staple cropland could supply need about 10\,TWp of modules, several times the present global fleet, and the best sites are rainfed maize and soybean areas where production-weighted retention is lowest.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Simulation only.} All results come from a simulator. PV output was benchmarked against PVGIS (29 sites, mean bias $-4.9$\%), but the crop response to shade was learned from one meta-analysis data set and compared with four field trials (Sections~\ref{sec:shade} and~\ref{sec:mlresults}). The simulated response is conservative with respect to the trials (rice, wheat, potato, maize) but too mild for maize and soybean relative to the meta-analysis, where the 40\%-shading relative yields are 0.42 and 0.40 against 0.75 and 0.48 in the simulator. The harvest-index sensitivities $h$ are assumptions, and the sensitivity analysis uses rule controllers on a subsample of 22 sites.
\item \emph{Canopy cooling.} The effect of shade on canopy temperature was represented by one coefficient $\kappa$, applied to a heat-stress function that was calibrated on air temperature \citep{siebert2014,webber2018}. The ranking of the controllers is robust to $\kappa$ between 0 and 2\,\textdegree C, but the absolute electricity changes by about $\pm8$\%. Nocturnal warming under modules and rain redistribution are not modelled.
\item \emph{Crop model.} SIMPLE has a fixed harvest index and linear radiation-use efficiency. More detailed models with grain-number formation and canopy energy balance may favour different schedules.
\item \emph{Climate scenarios.} Warming was applied as uniform temperature shifts with fixed precipitation, radiation and relative humidity, and with unchanged cultivars and sowing dates. It is not a bias-adjusted climate projection.
\item \emph{Initialisation and state information.} The network is initialised from the phenology rule, because training from random weights collapsed to full sharing. The gain of SALS is therefore the gain of fine-tuning that rule, and we cannot say whether a better optimiser or a richer policy class would find larger gains. The controller uses the simulated state of the crop and soil (thermal time, canopy interception, soil water) without measurement error, and the forecast errors we tested apply to weather only.
\item \emph{Economics and deployment.} There is no cost, market or grid analysis. The value of midday electricity, curtailment, storage, structural cost and farmer acceptance \citep{trommsdorff2021} will determine the realisable share. The average row pitch at the chosen densities (about 13\,m for 2.2-m modules) leaves room for machinery, but the densest designs (GCR up to 0.5) have a pitch of only 4.4\,m.
\end{itemize}
