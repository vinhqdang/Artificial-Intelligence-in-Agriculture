\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{What the results establish}
Four findings are discussed; the first three stand on consistent evidence and the fourth is not established. First, sharing light with the crop is the main lever on the electricity that tracker-based agrivoltaic arrays can produce under a yield floor. With static trackers the floor limits arrays to about 6\% ground coverage and an LER of 1.07. Any light-sharing control allows 2.1 to 2.9 times the density, and the LER rises to 1.18--1.24 for hand-written rules. Second, the best simple rule follows crop phenology. It shares light from the onset of canopy development (5\% of the thermal-time requirement) until maturity and produces electricity before and after, when the crop does not use the light. It needs only the crop's thermal time, which is observable on the farm, and it is insensitive to forecast errors, whereas the rule that reacts to heat and drought stress is worse in every climate and degrades with forecast error. Third, the qualitative result does not depend on the weakest part of the model. The simulated shade response is conservative against the field trials we could use (Section~\ref{sec:shade}); calibrating it raises the allowed density, and the phenology rule stays the best method, with a gain over static arrays of 0.17, 0.19 and 0.30 LER under the conservative, calibrated and field-fitted responses. However, the response learned from the meta-analysis shows steeper losses for maize and grain legumes than the simulator reproduces (Section~\ref{sec:mlresults}), so the absolute gains are conservative for wheat, rice and potato and uncertain, possibly overestimated, for maize and soybean (53\% of the upscaled electricity). Fourth, a controller learned through the differentiable simulator is better than the best rule we could write, by 0.044 in LER against the written rule but by only 0.007 (0.002 to 0.014; 2.4\% more electricity at equal retention, and not distinguishable from zero at matched retention) against a window optimised on the training seasons of each split. The margin over the written rule is consistent over training seeds (main split, range 0.002 in LER), the three spatial splits, three canopy-cooling assumptions and the baseline and $+3$\,\textdegree C climates, whereas the margin over the optimised window is small and varies between splits (0.008, 0.009 and 0.005 LER on splits 0, 1 and 2, mean of three training seeds; per-pair medians 0.001 to 0.002), so it is not established as a reproducible gain. This gain is small compared with the gap between static and dynamic control.

\subsection{What role does artificial intelligence play?}\label{sec:ai}
We tested three ways in which learning could matter, and the answers differ. The first is learning the crop's response to shade from data. The learned model is useful mainly as a measurement of how little can be learned: a hierarchical model fitted to 428 observations from 58 studies predicts a held-out study of the four staple crop types with an error of 0.22 in relative yield, no better than proportional loss, and the gain over proportional loss (0.43 to 0.31 over all types) comes from crop types that the simulator does not represent. Crop type is the information that transfers, whereas irradiation, climate zone and shade type add nothing measurable and a non-parametric model (gradient-boosted trees) is worse with this number of studies (Section~\ref{sec:mlresults}). Two things follow. The shade response of the staple crops is a distribution with a spread of about 20 percentage points between studies and not a curve, and the process-based simulator does not reproduce the steep losses of maize and legumes seen in the data. Learning the response was limited by the data and not by the model: two studies support the tuber curve, no rice study exists, and 46 panel observations are all leafy vegetables.

The second is calibration through the differentiable simulator. Gradient descent through whole seasons fitted the response of each crop to the learned curves and to the field trials, and it produced an ensemble of plausible responses that fits the trials and the curves for cereals and potato but not the maize and soybean curves (Section~\ref{sec:uncertain}). The ensemble informs the design decision: a density chosen under the mean response leaves about 70\% of crop--site pairs at the floor if another plausible response holds, against 85--87\% for an oracle, and a density chosen to meet the floor in 75\% of plausible responses recovers about half of that gap at a cost of 0.03 LER. A simple margin reaches similar compliance: raising the floor from 0.90 to 0.92 under the mean response reaches 85--88\% of the pairs, and interpolating the margin frontier at the compliance of the ensemble rules gives LER differences (margin minus rule) of $+0.001$ ($-0.009$ to $0.012$) and $+0.003$ ($-0.016$ to $0.021$) under E2 and $-0.006$ ($-0.019$ to $0.005$) and $+0.024$ ($-0.011$ to $0.058$) under E3, so the two are not distinguishable on this sample (equivalence is not established, since the intervals reach $\pm0.02$ to $0.06$ LER). None of the rules is safe outside the ensemble (7--33\% of the pairs at the floor under the proportional, calibrated and harsh responses for the mean design, the margin of 0.92 and the ensemble rules), and only a floor of 0.96 reaches 51--78\% at a cost of about 0.2 LER. What the ensemble adds is the size of the margin, not a better rule. The quantile rule is a scenario-type design \citep{calafiore2006,campi2008}; with five members it has none of the finite-sample guarantees of that theory, and since the comparison with the margin shows no detectable difference (with intervals too wide to establish equivalence), we see no evidence that it is needed. The bottleneck of tracker-based agrivoltaics under a yield-retention floor is the uncertainty of the crop response that enters the design, and it is data on that response, not larger networks, that would improve the result; the learned curves show a spread of about 20 percentage points between studies. The guarantee holds only within the range of responses that the ensemble spans.

The third is control, and here learning contributes little. Differentiability is what lets a network of 5441 weights be trained in 300 gradient steps through whole seasons; a derivative-free search such as CMA-ES handled a five-parameter schedule with 200 simulator evaluations per run and found an almost binary window that is only 0.007 LER behind SALS, but we did not test it on the network, whose 5441 weights would need gradient information. The learned controller adds 0.007 LER over an optimised window (and 0.017 over a grid-tuned one, 0.044 over the written rule), and 0.005--0.010 over the optimised window under the four alternative responses on one split (21 pairs, intervals containing zero); a reader who needs a simple, auditable controller can use that window and obtain about 84\% of the LER gain of SALS over the written rule. Neither of the two extensions that we expected to matter under uncertainty did: training on ensembles of responses (E1, E2 and E3) did not help and, for the wider ensembles, did not retain the margin over the written rule within our training budget (one seed, 21 pairs), and neither did giving the controller a farm-measurable signal, the biomass of the crop relative to a reference plot, nor a feedback rule built on that signal. The learned policy also has to be initialised from the phenology rule (Section~\ref{sec:limits}). We therefore do not claim that learned control is what makes agrivoltaics work: in this model, the schedule that a learned policy reaches is a smooth function of crop phenology that an optimised window already approximates to within 0.007 LER, and the source of the remaining margin is not identified.

\subsection{What the results do not establish}
The early hypothesis that stress-aware control, that is, shading the crop on heat and drought days, would be a main source of benefit under warming was not supported. The stress rule performs worse than the phenology rule in every climate, and a learned controller without stress inputs performs as well as one with them. A plausible reason is that in our crop model the yield gain from shading on a hot or dry day is smaller than the loss of light on that day, so stress information does not help. Whether this holds for crop models with more detailed canopy-temperature and heat-stress responses is an open question. Agrivoltaics does not protect staple production against warming in our simulations: light-sharing systems produce about 90\% of the open-field yield in every climate and follow the same decline in maize and rice (Fig.~\ref{fig:warming}).

\subsection{Food security in practice}
Holding the historical mean retention at 90\% does not hold it in each season or at each site. 70\% of SALS seasons meet the floor, the lower tail is slightly heavier than for the rules, and production-weighted retention is below 0.9 for soybean (0.888) and maize (0.898) and in several regions. Chance-constrained design raises season-level compliance to about 92\% at a cost of 0.065 in LER, and the ranking of the controllers is preserved. Whichever formulation is chosen, a per-site regulatory floor would need a region- and crop-specific design step. The upscaled potential (Section~\ref{sec:global}) shows the trade-off in magnitude. The 14\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$ that 5\% of the staple cropland could supply need about 10\,TWp of modules, several times the present global fleet, and the best sites are rainfed maize and soybean areas where production-weighted retention is lowest.

\textbf{Who bears the risk.} A floor defined on the multi-year mean leaves the farmer exposed in the seasons below it: with the density designed for the floor, about 30\% of seasons fall short on the main split (70\% compliance for SALS), the shortfall is mostly small (2.7\% of SALS seasons are below 0.8 of the open-field yield and the 10th percentile of retention is 0.883), and a per-season guarantee through chance-constrained design costs 0.065 LER. Siting by electricity favours maize and soybean areas, where compliance is lowest (Section~\ref{sec:global}), so the risk concentrates where the energy gain is largest. The crop revenue at stake (about 0.3 kUSD\,ha$^{-1}$\,yr$^{-1}$) is small against 20--30 kUSD of electricity revenue, so the exposure is mainly one of compliance with the floor and of the contract, and it coincides with the largest uncertainty of the shade response, that of maize and soybean. Who carries it, through contracts, insurance or a premium, is a design question that the model does not address, and food is valued only through crop revenue at market prices.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Simulation only.} All results come from a simulator. PV output was benchmarked against PVGIS (29 sites, mean bias $-4.9$\%), but the crop response to shade was learned from one meta-analysis data set and compared with four field trials (Sections~\ref{sec:shade} and~\ref{sec:mlresults}). The simulated response is conservative with respect to the field trials of rice, wheat and potato; for maize it is conservative with respect to the trials but milder than the meta-analysis, and for soybean the simulator (0.48 at 40\% shading) lies within the interval of the data (0.40, 80\% interval 0.34--0.53), while for maize the data give 0.42 against 0.75 in the simulator, so for these crops the direction of the bias depends on the source. The harvest-index sensitivities $h$ are assumptions, and the sensitivity analyses use subsamples of crop--site pairs (22 for the rule comparison under alternative responses, 21 for the controller comparison, one training seed).
\item \emph{Canopy cooling.} The effect of shade on canopy temperature was represented by one coefficient $\kappa$, applied to a heat-stress function that was calibrated on air temperature \citep{siebert2014,webber2018}. The ranking of the controllers is robust to $\kappa$ between 0 and 2\,\textdegree C, but the absolute electricity changes by about $\pm8$\%. Nocturnal warming under modules and rain redistribution are not modelled.
\item \emph{Crop model.} SIMPLE has a fixed harvest index and linear radiation-use efficiency, and its CO$_2$ response is that of controlled experiments that tend to overstate field responses \citep{long2006}. More detailed models with grain-number formation and canopy energy balance may favour different schedules.
\item \emph{Layouts and models.} The baseline of static arrays is a backtracking tracker held at the energy-optimal angle; flat and 30$^\circ$-rotated (east-facing, not equator-facing) fixed arrays did no better in a check on 21 pairs, but vertical bifacial layouts \citep{willockx2023,campana2021} are not modelled, and the ground coverage ratio is a coarse proxy of crop productivity \citep{dupraz2023}. The PV model is isotropic in the diffuse component and has no bifacial gain; it was benchmarked against PVGIS at low density and against pvlib \citep{holmgren2018} at ground coverage ratios up to 0.5, where it underestimates the shading loss by about 1.5 to 2.5 points. The rotation limit was varied between 45 and 80 degrees and changes the size of the benefit but not the ranking; the harvest-index window is assumed to be common to all crops, and shifting it or removing the penalty did not change the ranking of the rules.
\item \emph{Climate scenarios.} Warming was applied as uniform temperature shifts with fixed precipitation, radiation and relative humidity, and with unchanged cultivars and sowing dates. It is not a bias-adjusted climate projection.
\item \emph{Initialisation and state information.} The network is initialised from the phenology rule, because training from random weights collapsed to full sharing. The gain of SALS is therefore the gain of fine-tuning that rule, and we cannot say whether a better optimiser or a richer policy class would find larger gains. The controller uses the simulated state of the crop and soil (thermal time, canopy interception, soil water) without measurement error, and the forecast errors we tested apply to weather only.
\item \emph{Economics and deployment.} The economic analysis (Section~\ref{sec:cost}) uses assumed prices and costs, no grid, curtailment, storage or subsidy model, and does not value food security; its break-even prices move with the capex and the discount rate (Fig.~\ref{fig:cost}). The value of midday electricity, curtailment, storage, structural cost and farmer acceptance \citep{trommsdorff2021} will determine the realisable share. The average row pitch at the chosen densities (about 13\,m for 2.2-m modules) leaves room for machinery, but the densest designs (GCR up to 0.5) have a pitch of only 4.4\,m.
\item \emph{Floor and field effects.} The floor of 0.9 is a modelling convention and not a regulatory threshold (DIN SPEC 91434 uses 66\%, Supplementary Material). No field validation under trackers exists for the controllers, and the simulator has no emergence, early canopy closure, weed or machinery effects; the PV model has not been compared with measured array output at ground coverage above 0.05, only with pvlib.
\end{itemize}
