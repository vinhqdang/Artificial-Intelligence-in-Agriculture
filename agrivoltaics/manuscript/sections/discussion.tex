\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Why learning the control matters}
The results show that most of the electricity that agrivoltaics can add without compromising food production comes from \emph{when} light is shared rather than from the array geometry alone. With static trackers, the yield floor forces sparse arrays that cover a tenth of the ground. Rules that share light during the season double the electricity, and a learned, state-dependent schedule nearly triples it. SALS concentrates the crop's light in the phases where intercepted radiation is converted into biomass most efficiently (canopy build-up) and when light, rather than heat or water, limits growth. In the remaining days it produces electricity and shades the crop against heat. This agrees with field observations that shading is less damaging, or even beneficial, when it coincides with heat or drought \citep{marrou2013,barron2019,weselek2021}, and with the dynamic-tracking experiments of \citet{valle2017}. SALS generalises this idea across crops and climates through a policy learned from data rather than from hand-crafted rules.

Differentiable simulation is what makes this learning tractable. A season has 365 coupled decisions and a single delayed outcome, the harvest. Exact gradients through the physics give credit to each daily decision without the variance of sampled returns that makes reinforcement learning with black-box crop simulators expensive \citep{gautron2022}. Training one policy on about 4000 seasons in four climates took about 40 minutes on four CPU cores, and the policy transfers to unseen regions and years. Interestingly, the policy outperformed per-season trajectory optimisation with perfect foresight. Sharing one policy across thousands of seasons regularises the non-convex control problem, a benefit of amortised optimisation.

\subsection{Implications for energy security and climate adaptation}
Agrivoltaics has been advocated as a way to decarbonise electricity without taking land from food \citep{dinesh2016,adeh2019}. Our results quantify this potential under an explicit food-security floor and show how strongly it depends on control. With static designs, 5\% of the area of five staple crops would supply about 40\% of today's global electricity. With learned light sharing it would supply roughly all of it, on land that keeps producing 93\% of its food. The potential grows with warming because heat makes shade less costly. Agrivoltaics therefore becomes more attractive as the climate warms, in the regions of South Asia, Africa and the Americas where both electricity demand and heat risks for crops are rising. SALS does not, however, reverse the negative impact of warming on the aggregate yields of maize and rice. Agrivoltaics complements breeding, irrigation and management adaptation; it does not replace them.

\subsection{Limitations}\label{sec:limits}
Several simplifications bound the conclusions. (i) The crop model is generic. SIMPLE has a linear radiation-use efficiency, so it does not reward the higher light-use efficiency of shaded canopies, and it represents heat stress through maximum temperature only. Crop-specific shade responses \citep{marrou2013,weselek2021} and multi-model ensembles \citep{jagermeyr2021} should be used to confirm the results. (ii) The microclimate model is empirical: the canopy-cooling coefficient $\kappa$ strongly affects yield retention (Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity}) and needs field calibration for trackers. (iii) Warming was applied as uniform temperature shifts with fixed relative humidity, precipitation and irradiance, not as bias-adjusted climate-model projections, and cultivars and sowing dates were not adapted. (iv) Fields are either rainfed or fully irrigated, and irrigation water savings under the panels are not credited. (v) SALS meets the yield floor on average but not in every season. A chance-constrained or risk-averse objective, e.g.\ a conditional value-at-risk of the retention, is needed where the floor is enforced every year. (vi) The upscaled potentials are technical: grid capacity, storage, the value of midday electricity, the cost of elevated structures and farmers' acceptance \citep{trommsdorff2021} will determine the realisable share.
