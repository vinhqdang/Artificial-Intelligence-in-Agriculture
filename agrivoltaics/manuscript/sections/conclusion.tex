\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
Light sharing, the daily choice between maximum electricity and letting light through to the crop, is the main lever on the electricity of tracker-based agrivoltaics at a given yield-retention floor, although it pays only at high electricity prices: below about 80\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ static arrays earn more. In a differentiable simulator of trackers, PV output, crop light, microclimate and crop growth, evaluated on held-out crop--site pairs (92 on the main split, 76 distinct pairs pooled over three splits for the controller and design comparisons) with the array density of each site chosen from its own history, light-sharing rules allowed 2.1 to 2.9 times the module density of static arrays at a mean yield retention of 90\%. They raised the land-equivalent ratio from 1.07 to 1.18--1.24, and a rule that follows crop phenology and needs no weather forecast was the best of them. The simulated shade response is conservative against the field trials we could use for rice, wheat and potato, and calibrating it raised the gains without changing the ranking of methods; for maize and soybean, which supply 53\% of the upscaled electricity, the meta-analysis indicates larger losses than the simulator reproduces whereas one maize trial indicates smaller ones, so the gains for these crops are uncertain and possibly overestimated. The crop's response to shade, the least certain input, was learned from 428 field observations: a hierarchical model predicted a held-out study of the staple crop types no better than proportional loss (error 0.22), which shows the size of the between-study spread, and gradient descent through the simulator turned it into an ensemble of plausible responses.  A learned controller exceeded the written phenology rule by 0.044 LER and a rule with a window tuned on the training seasons by 0.018 (95\% interval 0.012--0.024), by following a smooth phenology-dependent schedule that a window or a ramp rule approximates within 0.013 LER (the rest may come from its state inputs such as soil water and density, which we did not test); training it on ensembles of shade responses or giving it a farm-measurable biomass signal did not help, and with the wider ensembles it did not retain its margin over the written rule in our training budget (one seed, 21 pairs). The share of pairs at the floor varied by up to 18 points between design rules (70\% to 88\%) and by 4 points between controllers (87\% to 91\% among light-sharing controllers), so the design step carried more compliance risk than the control. A density chosen under the mean response left about 70\% of crop--site pairs at the floor when another plausible response held (oracle 85--87\%); choosing the density so that the floor holds in 75\% of the plausible responses recovered about half of the gap at a cost of 0.03 LER, and a floor raised by 0.02 under the mean response gave no detectable difference at equal compliance (differences of $-0.006$ to $+0.024$ LER with intervals containing zero, too wide to establish equivalence); no rule was safe outside the ensemble. The ensemble is therefore useful to size a safety margin, not to replace it. Under illustrative costs, light sharing breaks even with the open field at 70 to 92\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ (73 for the learned controller, 59 for static arrays); a conventional plant replacing the crop is more profitable above its break-even of 48\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$, so agrivoltaics depends on a premium, land-use rules or a value for food production that markets do not give. The yield floor is met on average but not in every season or region, and the electricity potential of 5\% of staple cropland (about 14\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$) requires about 10\,TWp of modules. Future work should validate the crop response to shade under trackers with more field data, notably for maize and legumes where the meta-analysis and the trials disagree, extend the crop model to grain-number formation and canopy energy balance, add costs and grid constraints, and pursue controllers that guarantee the yield-retention floor per site and per season.
