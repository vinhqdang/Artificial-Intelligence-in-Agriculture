\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusion}
Light sharing, the daily choice between maximum electricity and letting light through to the crop, is the main lever on the electricity of tracker-based agrivoltaics at a given yield-retention floor. In a differentiable simulator, evaluated on held-out crop--site pairs (92 on the main split, 76 distinct pairs pooled over three splits for the controller and design comparisons) with the density of each site chosen from its own history, light-sharing rules allowed 2.1 to 2.9 times the module density of static arrays at a mean yield retention of 90\% and raised the land-equivalent ratio from 1.07 to 1.18--1.24 under the proportional-growth shade response. A rule that follows crop phenology and needs no weather forecast was the best written rule. The lever pays only at high electricity prices: with illustrative costs it breaks even with the open field, at floor-designed densities, at 70 to 92\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$ (73 for the learned controller, 59 for static arrays), static arrays earn more below about 75 to 85\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$, and a conventional plant replacing the crop is more profitable above its break-even of 48\,USD\,MWh$^{-1}$. In this model agrivoltaics therefore pays only with a premium, land-use rules or a value for food production that the model does not include.

The crop's response to shade, the least certain input, was learned from 428 field observations. For the staple crop types a hierarchical model predicted a held-out study no better than proportional loss (error 0.22), which measures the between-study spread, and gradient descent through the simulator turned the learned curves and four field trials into ensembles of plausible responses. The simulator is conservative against the trials for rice, wheat and potato; for maize and soybean, which supply 53\% of the upscaled electricity, the meta-analysis indicates larger losses than simulated and one maize trial smaller ones, so the gains for these crops are uncertain and possibly overestimated. A learned controller exceeded the written phenology rule by 0.044 LER but a CMA-ES-optimised window by only 0.007 (95\% interval 0.002--0.014, mean of three training seeds per split; at matched retention 0.003, interval $-0.0004$ to 0.007). The source of this margin is not identified: it is not explained by the tuning of a window or a ramp, and masking soil water, aridity and forecasts leaves the controller unchanged. Training on ensembles of shade responses, or giving the controller a farm-measurable biomass signal, did not help within our budget (one seed, 21 pairs).

Compliance depends more on the response assumed in the design than on the controller. A density chosen under the mean response left about 70\% of crop--site pairs at the floor when another plausible response held, against 85--87\% for an oracle design; this gap of 15 points (a model-misspecification effect) is larger than the 4 points between light-sharing controllers at a fixed design rule under a known response, but the two are not like-for-like. Designing for the floor in 75\% of the plausible responses recovered about half of the gap at a cost of 0.03 LER, and a floor raised by 0.02 under the mean response gave no detectable difference at equal compliance (the intervals include zero and are too wide to establish equivalence); no rule was safe outside the ensemble, whose members differ less from one another than a new study differs from the learned curve. The ensemble is therefore useful to size a safety margin, not to replace it. The yield floor is met on average but not in every season or region, and the electricity potential of 5\% of staple cropland (about 14\,PWh\,yr$^{-1}$, a scaling convention without an interval) requires about 10\,TWp of modules. Future work should validate the crop response to shade under trackers with more field data, notably for maize and legumes where the meta-analysis and the trials disagree, extend the crop model to grain-number formation and canopy energy balance, add costs and grid constraints, and pursue controllers that guarantee the yield-retention floor per site and per season.
