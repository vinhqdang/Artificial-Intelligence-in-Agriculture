\section{Materials and methods}\label{sec:methods}
The full description, with all equations, parameters and training details, is in the Supplementary Material (Section~\ref{S-sec:supp-methods}); this section gives what is needed to follow the results.

\subsection{Sites, weather and scenarios}\label{sec:m-data}
We studied five staple crops (wheat, rice, maize, soybean, potato; about 670\,Mha of harvested area). Harvested areas came from SPAM 2010 \citep{yu2020}. For each crop, 80 one-degree cells with at least 5000\,ha were drawn with probability proportional to harvested area, so that an unweighted mean over the 400 crop--site pairs approximates an area-weighted global mean (Fig.~\ref{fig:m-map}). Sowing and maturity dates came from the GGCMI Phase~3 crop calendar \citep{jagermeyr2021} and daily weather for 2001--2020 from NASA POWER \citep{sparks2018}. Each site contributes 19 seasons (7600 site-seasons), each simulated for the 365 days after sowing. Warming levels of +1.5, +2 and +3\,\textdegree C were imposed by shifting daily temperatures uniformly, with CO$_2$ of 450, 500 and 600\,ppm (390\,ppm in the baseline); precipitation and irradiance were unchanged.

\subsection{A differentiable agrivoltaic simulator}\label{sec:m-sim}
The simulator (Fig.~\ref{fig:m-scheme}) advances all site-seasons in parallel, with a daily step for the crop and an hourly step for radiation and PV, in PyTorch \citep{paszke2019}, so that yield and electricity are differentiable with respect to design and control variables. We considered elevated horizontal single-axis trackers with north--south axes, an axis height of 1.25 module widths, a rotation limit of $\pm60^\circ$ and $g\le0.5$. With backtracking rotation $\phi_{\mathrm{bt}}$ \citep{lorenzo2011,marion2013} and the rotation $\phi_{\mathrm{sh}}$ that minimises the shadow on the crop, a daily \emph{light-sharing level} $u\in[0,1]$ sets the hourly rotation as
\begin{equation}
\phi=(1-u)\,\phi_{\mathrm{bt}}+u\,\phi_{\mathrm{sh}}.
\label{eq:phi}
\end{equation}
Plane-of-array irradiance combines beam light reduced by row-to-row shading, isotropic sky diffuse and ground-reflected light; electricity follows from module efficiency (0.21), the NOCT cell temperature and 14\% system losses, and a conventional ground-mounted plant ($g=0.4$) is the energy reference $E_{\mathrm{PV}}$. Beam and diffuse light at the crop follow from the geometry (the diffuse part from a pre-computed two-dimensional sky-view factor), and the canopy maximum temperature is lowered by $\kappa(1-s)$ with $s$ the share of open-field irradiance that reaches the crop and a moderate $\kappa=1$\,\textdegree C (sensitivity: $\kappa=0$ and 2\,\textdegree C). Reference evapotranspiration is recomputed under the modules \citep{allen1998}. Crop growth follows SIMPLE \citep{zhao2019},
\begin{equation}
\Delta B=R_{\mathrm{crop}}\cdot\mathrm{RUE}\cdot f_{\mathrm{Solar}}\cdot f_{\mathrm{CO_2}}\cdot f_{\mathrm{Temp}}\cdot\min\{f_{\mathrm{Heat}},f_{\mathrm{Water}}\},
\label{eq:simple}
\end{equation}
with $R_{\mathrm{crop}}=sR$ the radiation at the crop, a soil-water balance for drought stress, a thermal-time requirement calibrated per site from the crop calendar, and each site split into a rainfed and an irrigated sub-field. SIMPLE has a fixed harvest index, so we added a penalty for shade around flowering: in the window $0.45\le\mathrm{TT}/T_{\mathrm{sum}}\le0.65$ the harvest index is multiplied by $1-h\bar d$, with $\bar d$ the mean radiation deficit and a sensitivity $h$ of 0.6 for maize, 0.5 for soybean and rice, 0.4 for wheat and 0.2 for potato (assumptions that reproduce the ranking of crop types in the data). The PV part agrees with PVGIS at low density ($r=0.97$ at 29 sites, simulator 4.9\% lower) and was compared with pvlib \citep{holmgren2018} at higher density (Section~\ref{sec:m-valid}).

\subsection{Light-sharing control, design and baselines}\label{sec:m-control}
SALS is a multilayer perceptron (two hidden layers of 64 units) that sets $u$ each day from the crop and soil state, noisy forecasts of maximum temperature, irradiance and ET$_0$ (errors of 2\,\textdegree C, 20\% and 15\%), the irrigation status, the density, the latitude, the day of season and the crop. It is trained by back-propagation through the 365 daily steps of the simulator to minimise $-e+\mu_1\max\{0,\rho-r\}+\mu_2\max\{0,\rho-r\}^2$ with $r$ the yield retention, $e$ the electricity relative to a conventional plant and $\rho=0.9$, after behaviour cloning of the phenology rule (trained from random weights it converges to the seasonal-sharing rule). Its gain over the phenology rule therefore measures what fine-tuning through the simulator adds to that rule.

Every method uses the same \emph{history-based design rule}: the density of a site is the largest value on the grid (step 0.025 in the main split, 0.05 in the pooled analyses) for which the mean retention over its 2001--2014 seasons is at least 0.9; if none qualifies, the site stays open field. Test seasons (2015--2019) are never used for design, and test sites never for training. We compare static arrays (backtracking, $u=0$), seasonal sharing ($u=1$ throughout the season), a stress rule (sharing except on forecast heat or drought days), a written phenology rule (sharing for $0.05\le\mathrm{TT}/T_{\mathrm{sum}}\le1$), a window tuned on the training seasons of each split (grid-tuned and CMA-ES optimised \citep{hansen2001}; Supplementary Material) and SALS, with a conventional plant and the open field as references.

\subsection{Shade response: data, ensembles and design under uncertainty}\label{sec:m-uncertainty}
The crop response to shade was learned from 428 observations of 58 studies \citep{laub2022} as $y(s)=\exp(-\beta s^{\gamma})$ with partial pooling over crop types and compared by leave-one-study-out cross-validation with proportional loss $y=1-s$ and with gradient-boosted trees. The simulator has two shade parameters per crop, a radiation-use compensation and a multiplier on the harvest-index penalty. Because the simulator is differentiable, they were fitted by gradient descent through whole seasons to the learned curves (20\% and 40\% shading) and to field trials on rice, wheat, potato and maize \citep{gonocruz2021,weselek2021,ramosfuentes2023}. Repeating the fit on randomly perturbed observations gave an ensemble of plausible responses (E2; the Supplement also uses a wider ensemble E3). The members differ by a standard deviation of 0.02--0.06 in relative yield, generally less than the between-study error of 0.22.

The density under uncertainty is chosen as (i) the \emph{mean-response design}, the design rule under the mean of the members; (ii) a \emph{margin}, the same rule with the floor raised to $d>0.9$ under the mean response; (iii) an \emph{ensemble rule} that requires the floor in a share of the members; (iv) a \emph{conformal margin}, the smallest $d$ whose conformal-adjusted risk of missing the floor, $n/(n+1)\,\hat R(d)+1/(n+1)$, is at most a target $\alpha$ \citep{angelopoulos2024}, with the pair as the unit, calibrated on the pairs of two splits that are not in the third and tested on the third; and (v) a \emph{learned design network}, a small multilayer perceptron that chooses a density from the history retention curves of the other members (mean, minimum and maximum at each density), the crop and the latitude, trained to minimise $-\mathrm{LER}+\lambda\,\mathbf{1}[r<0.9]$ under the held-out member, with the same leave-one-split-out evaluation (details in the Supplement). Pairs held out in several splits are averaged. The truth is a held-out member, or one of four fixed responses (proportional, calibrated, field-fitted and harsh; only the field-fitted lies within the range of the ensemble) with the belief the mean of the ensemble's 12 members, and an oracle design under the true response is the reference.

\subsection{Economics and evaluation}\label{sec:m-eval}
Annual net value per hectare relative to the open field is electricity revenue minus the annuity and operating cost of the installed capacity ($2.1\,g$\,MWp\,ha$^{-1}$) minus the lost crop revenue; a conventional plant ($g=0.4$) replacing the crop is the comparator. All parameters are assumptions (Supplementary Table~\ref{S-tab:costparams}), so we report break-even electricity prices and sensitivities. Sites were split spatially (10\textdegree{} blocks, 70\% training, 30\% test) with three splits; training used 2001--2014 and evaluation the 2015--2019 seasons of held-out sites, and results are for 92 held-out pairs (main split) or 76 distinct pairs pooled over three splits. Metrics are electricity, mean and production-weighted retention, compliance (share of seasons or sites with retention $\ge0.9$), and the land-equivalent ratio LER $=r+e$ \citep{dupraz2011}, relative to the open field of the same climate. Intervals are cluster bootstrap intervals over crop--site pairs; SALS was trained with three seeds per split. For the global estimate, each crop's mean electricity per hectare is scaled to its physical area in SPAM 2010.
